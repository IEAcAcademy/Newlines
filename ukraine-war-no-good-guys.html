There Are No Good Guys in this War!

March 10, 2022

It has been nearly two weeks since the war began in Ukraine, a country ten times larger than Sri Lanka and with twice its population. In many ways, its experience seems similar to Sri Lanka's – except that Ukraine has 15 nuclear power plants that could be targets in this conflict, raising fears of a nuclear catastrophe.

Let me be clear: I stand with the Ukrainian and Russian people who are heroically resisting this war, and I support all those protesting the unbearable and often unnecessary burden this war imposes on civilians. If there is one lesson we've learned over the past decade, it's that there are no winners in war, and we all lose something beyond our humanity.

Condemning aggression is a necessary step. Putin's ultranationalist ideology of restoring Russia to its Tsarist-era 'glory' is widely documented in Western media. However, less visible in this coverage is the hawkish nationalism of Ukrainian presidents and their refusal to pursue diplomatic solutions in favor of military action since 2014. Mainstream media also largely overlooks the ongoing, aggressive search by the US and its allies for enemies or causes to justify NATO's existence after the Soviet Union's collapse. This war results from these three forces.

I want to emphasize that nothing justifies invasion. However, to prevent potentially catastrophic and largely preventable conflicts like these in the future, we need to understand how we reached this point.

Media Coverage

Yes, it is not the media that launched this war. Yet the Western media appears to have abdicated its responsibility. Instead of presenting the whole truth to nudge their governments to take diplomatic options seriously, they presented half-truths. They cherry-picked facts to justify the war that the ruling ideology of the West is not too keen to avoid.

There are two opposing schools of thought regarding the backdrop of this war: Some argue that the present situation explains why Europe needs NATO, while others believe that NATO should not have continued to exist after the demise of the Soviet Union. Remnants of Cold War ideology created the much-needed enemy to justify NATO and led to this war - in other words, the existence of NATO will generate the reasons for its existence.

I support the latter hypothesis based on the available evidence. None of the US mainstream media covered the historic Russian concerns - the exceptions, like Democracy Now! and the Nation, are too few and have a limited audience. Instead, the media continues to play a partisan role by demonizing Putin and focusing exclusively on his aggression while avoiding the context that seems to fuel it. In short, they continue to act as the voice of warmongers.

Russian-Sponsored Civil War and Ukrainian Intransigence

The Revolution of Dignity in Ukraine [mainly in Kyiv] was triggered by the 2013 decision of the pro-Russian President Yanukovych not to sign the EU-Ukraine Association Agreement (which would have paved the way for Ukraine to join the EU). Instead, he chose to embrace the Eurasian Economic Union favored by Russia. In the end, Yanukovych was forced out of office (he fled to Russia). Subsequently, Russia annexed Crimea in 2014 and instigated and continued to support an uprising in the southern and eastern parts of Ukraine (LPR and DPR).

Facing this aggression, the Government of Ukraine pursued a military option with active support from the US and its allies. For instance, since 2014, Ukraine has received $2.7 billion in military assistance from the US. In 2014, President Petro Poroshenko incorporated the neo-Nazi militia Azov into the National Guard and praised their valour. Ukraine banned the use of the Russian language, including in the primarily Russian-speaking Southern and Eastern regions. Pursuing diplomatic options was not in the cards. The civil war that ensued has taken over 15,000 lives since 2014.

This continued even as the Russian invasion unfolded. The current President banned men of ages between18-60 from fleeing the country; he requested accelerated entry into NATO; and continued to pressure NATO to enforce a no-fly zone above Ukraine. The last one would have escalated this conflict into a European war. A total abdication of statesmanship accompanies the reckless disregard for civilian lives – the statesmanship needed to manage an aggressive neighbour. Sadly, this lack is celebrated as heroism by the champions of this war in the EU parliament and the Western media.

International Anti-war Activism: All Lives Matter

The outpouring of support for the Ukrainian people around the globe is heartening. A colleague of mine shared a message on Saturday asking me to switch off the lights at 8:00 pm for a while—a symbolic No to energy supplies from Putin’s Russia. I marveled at the creativity and symbolism. As I turned off the lights at the agreed time, I pondered the ongoing wars and refugee crises. Gradually, the war in Yemen drifted into my consciousness. It is not too far from Ukraine and has a similar population (30 million to Ukraine's 43). This war also started around the same time—2014–15—and has claimed over 18,500 lives. Civilians are targeted in this war as well—for example, the UK-based charity Oxfam noted that in January 2022 alone, there were 48 bombing attacks by the Saudi-led coalition on civilian targets. According to Save the Children, January also saw record deaths and casualties—599. How many of us would know the extent of the deliberate targeting of civilians in Yemen? If we knew that, would we consider a day of boycotting Saudi oil (not driving cars)? More importantly, how difficult would it be for Yemeni refugees to get asylum in Europe?

I deeply admire the courage and activism of the 13,500 Russians who have been incarcerated so far for protesting against the war, despite the harsh measures imposed by Putin. I raise this point not to argue that the international community should pay less attention to this war and the plight of Ukrainian civilians, but to call for recognition of our own biases, scrutiny of our choices, examination of the extent of our alignment with the International Order, and a reminder that atrocities committed against those who do not resemble us are no less heinous and that we should share our attention equitably.

The Outcomes of this War

It is doubtful that absolute victors and losers will emerge at the end of this war. Russia would like to force Ukraine to revert to its satellite status of the Soviet era, which would require occupation. It would want to make an example of Ukraine for the other countries in its 'sphere of influence'. In its version of the International Order, they all need to reverse their course toward joining the EU and avoid hostile military alliances (no NATO, no presence of hostile missiles, no military advisors, etc.). On the other hand, the US and its allies would like to enforce their vision of the Unipolar World Order and assert their monopoly on violence through punitive sanctions so comprehensive that they will deter Russia from pursuing this war and other acts of aggression without their sanction.

When the dust settles, we will likely see neither side fully achieving its goals. For instance, Russia should recognize by now that reverting to the Soviet-era situation is not feasible. Given the resistance it faces in Ukraine, installing a puppet regime is not a viable long-term option. It may have to settle for Ukraine's neutrality (no joining NATO or hosting hostile long-range missiles). And the West will recognize the limitations of sanctions against a determined economic and military power.

The West and Ukraine have to come to terms with the fact that even severe sanctions are unlikely to yield quick results. Sanctions will hurt Russians considerably in the long run, but Biden’s current call for all countries to ban oil imports from Russia is wrong-headed. It neglects the lessons from the OPEC oil embargo in the ‘70s, which resulted in a global recession and prolonged stagflation. Already, economies are reeling from the COVID shock. An oil price shock is the last thing the globe needs now.

While it's hard to predict exactly how things will change, some areas of potential change are worth considering. The media punditry is too busy war-mongering to worry about such trivialities; hence we have to do it ourselves.

A Unipolar International Order

The first casualty could well be the existing Unipolar International Order, which uses collective military and economic might to maintain a monopoly over global governance of trade and finance and to set the rules of the ‘game’. While there may be no radical changes immediately, the tendency to fray at the edges will accelerate. Militarily, Russia already challenged this Order in Syria, rather successfully (much to the detriment of the Syrian people). But the Order believes in the longer-term power of sanctions over short-term military losses.

Europe may bolster its defense (diverting resources from the urgent fight against climate change) and eventually provide an alternative to NATO/US dominance. Already, Trump (or one of his clones) is knocking at the doors of the US presidency in 2024. Trump has never hidden his admiration for Putin. Europe can no longer equate its ‘security/geopolitical’ concerns with those of the US. Recent statements from Germany point to its willingness to assert itself.

To what extent will lessons from this war embolden China and better prepare it to challenge the Order as it advances its agenda of expansion in the South China Sea and Taiwan? In other words, to what extent will this war empower China to test its might as a world power as it accelerates toward becoming the largest economy in the world?

To what extent other ex-Soviet bloc countries will be able to pursue an independent path to join the EU and NATO in the future? This is linked to a number of factors:

  • To what extent will Europe be willing to put boots on the ground to defend these countries when Putin sends his tanks? NATO actions have made it clear to Ukraine and any potential newcomers that it will not fight for them. While sanctions take time to take effect, the consequences of war are immediate and harsh.
  • The level of resistance Russia encountered during this invasion could make him realize that his original plan to occupy Ukraine and install a puppet regime was not viable. Sooner or later, he may have to settle for a ‘neutrality’ position for Ukraine and withdraw from the country. This would mean that Ukraine agrees not to join NATO or host hostile missiles against Russia, but will not join the.
  • If he succeeds in Ukraine, to what extent will Ukraine be a deterrent for the ex-Soviet bloc? If it doesn't function well enough as a deterrent, will Russia exercise further military aggression?
  • A relevant question is: Will Putin be able to repeat such aggression in the future, given the internal resistance he faces to these aggressive expansionary tactics? Unlike the Crimean war, which had broad support within Russia, a war with Ukraine appears to have had little popular support before the invasion. With rising casualties, this opposition will continue to mount – Russia has admitted to losing 500 soldiers so far, but the actual figure could be much higher; despite strict censorship (media is not allowed to use the word 'war' to describe the invasion) and draconian measures to suppress dissent, public protests are erupting in Russia – last Sunday alone, 5,000 protesters were arrested, bringing the total to over 13,500; even Putin's allies, such as the Russian Orthodox Church, have come out in opposition to the war. Finally, the war might have disabused Putin of the illusion that the peoples of these countries are interested in joining his grand Russian empire.

Towards a Bipolar Global Financial System – Dollar and Renminbi

A second impact will be further gains for the Renminbi as a reserve currency, though the dollar remains the dominant global currency. Russia and similarly sanction-prone countries may seek to decouple from the dollar and pursue alternatives to protect their assets. Since the 2014 sanctions following the illegal annexation of Crimea, Russia has diversified its $630 billion in liquid reserves - it reallocated over 55% of its reserves, and the Renminbi has benefited from this shift. As the largest trading nation and the second-largest economy, China’s push to make the Renminbi an alternative reserve currency will be boosted by the spurt of sanctions we have seen over the past two decades.

Collateral Damage

Rising fossil fuel prices will have the effect of strengthening Russia at the expense of the rest of Europe. Despite the ruble plummeting 35% (and Russian stock markets getting pummeled), crude prices have climbed well over $100 per barrel and, if 2014 is any indication, could go north of $150 per barrel. Unless Iran sanctions are lifted shortly and Saudi Arabia can be persuaded to ramp up its oil production, supply is not likely to increase. Therefore, prices will remain high throughout the war.

The recent move by the US and UK to impose a ban on Russian oil will have minimal effect on Russia's oil and gas revenues, since imports from the US and UK are miniscule compared with its supply to Europe. However, record-level gas prices in the US may cost Biden the midterm elections (which would have happened anyway). Many are betting on longer-term inflation in the US as a result of higher gas prices. If this were to happen, it may cost Biden his next presidential election.

Higher fossil fuel prices will tend to push up the prices of many essential items, including food. Moreover, Russia and Ukraine together account for a quarter of the global wheat trade. Even the more affluent nations will see their central banks struggling to cope with already high inflationary pressures, with little wiggle room to combat this shock. But the biggest casualty of this war, besides the millions of Ukrainian civilians, will be the poor across the world, particularly those in poorer nations whose economies are already buckling under the COVID shock – an additional 122 million people were pushed below the poverty line during the last two years, reversing the trend in the previous decade. In the coming months, the current hike in fuel and food prices is likely to push millions more into abject poverty and starvation.

In Summary: The existing International Order is a loose bundle of rules established by the powerful countries. This Order is blind to how it affects the lives of those in less powerful nations. Putin is correct to call out the hypocrisy of the current Unipolar Order (America and its allies calling all the shots), as evidenced by the invasion of Iraq (remember weapons of mass destruction?), Afghanistan, the War on Terror, and the list goes on. These instances reveal the fundamental nature of the Order – it is always about their immediate interests—Right and Wrong matter only when they are not too inconvenient for the powerful players' interests. Putin's Russia wants to be recognized as part of this club as before. The plight of Ukrainians is collateral damage in this 'jostling for position.' This will not be the first nor the last such instance.

As Dylan asked six decades ago, how long must wars be waged before they are finally banned?
