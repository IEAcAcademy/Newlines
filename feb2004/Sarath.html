Interviews · February 2004

Interview: Sarath Fernando

Co-Secretary, Movement for National Land and Agricultural Reform (MONLAR)

lines co-editor Ahilan Kadirgamar interviewed Mr. Sarath Fernando, Co-Secretary of Movement for National Land and Agricultural Reform (MONLAR) in January 2004. Mr. Sarath Fernando and MONLAR have been a critical voice since 1990 in creating awareness and mobilizing peoples movements around economic issues, particularly in the face of an onslaught of neo-liberal reforms. Their work over the past decades have led them to resist not only the policies of the Sri Lankan government but also the policies of multilateral organizations such as the World Bank and IMF. They were instrumental in forming the Alliance for Protection of National Resources and Human Rights (ANRHR) a broad coalition to resist the escalation of neo-liberal economic policies that are part of Regaining Sri Lanka, the economic development program introduced two years ago. The interview is not only about Mr. Sarath Fernando’s analysis of the political economy of Sri Lanka, but also delves into the history of the various political formations in the South such as the Old Left, UNP, SLFP and JVP, and their impact on class politics and economic policy.

Part I

AK: You have an interesting history and you come from an interesting generation of the Left. Can you speak about your early politicisation and your role in the first JVP struggle?

Sarath: I come from a Left background. My father was a member of the LSSP when it started in the 1930s and he remained a member till he died. When we left the Old Left and joined the New Left, he remained with the Old Left. When I was in the university in the late sixties, many young people began to be critical of the former Left movements. Because 1965 was the time when the dominant Left parties went into alliances with the SLFP. They formed these united coalitions. That was seen by the more radical organizations, and youth in particular, as reformism. Compromising ones ideological positions. Therefore, there was an effort among the young people to form their own militant, or what was called revolutionary political parties. Many of the New Left parties emerged out of Shanmugathasan’s Maoist party. Then there were many other initiatives by young people. I was at the university at that time, working with the communist party, but many young people left the party and became members of the JVP. Therefore, our becoming part of the JVP was to create a more radical revolutionary Left tendency. So, that is how I became a member of the JVP. I was doing civil engineering at the university, I completed my studies in 1969, but did not sit for my exams, started working fulltime with the JVP.

What was attractive about the JVP was that compared to the more traditional approaches or analysis of the traditional Left parties, the JVP tried to make a more concrete analysis of the Sri Lankan case. For instance, they had their five lessons. First was about the economic crisis in Sri Lanka, which was an attempt to make a concrete analysis. Now that was attractive. Also, the other attraction was the same criticism made by Shanmugadasan, that the Old Left had become reformist, that it has become parliamentarian, that it has gone for a compromise, which was true. But a total rejection of all that was done by the Old Left was the position taken by the JVP. Then, we were young people with an amount of immaturity. But today I think with that kind of perception and that kind of history of the Left movement, there is a very clear cleavage. The new young left had completely isolated itself form the historical Left movement in the country. That has created a division of complete lack of confidence and faith, which has been detrimental to not only the Old Left but also to the New Left, because they don’t have a history.

Now, that history of the Left movement. What was done by the Marxist parties from the 1930’s, while one can be critical of them, one has also to admit, that they have created a base for socialism. They have created a whole analysis and thinking about social justice. That is the contribution that the Left movement made in Sri Lanka in terms of democratising society, building workers movement, the trade union movement and its role in protecting the rights of the people. But all that was rejected. Of course young people accepted this rejection because of their inexperience, but also because of their desire to do something new. Something completely different.

There was something else that JVP had, about which we now strongly disagree. They also tried to make a whole issue out of Indian Expansionism. What JVP said at that time was, it was very similar to the Chinese critique, that India was trying to become a power in the region. Perhaps there is some truth in it. India’s expansionism was not to bring about socialism, but for their capitalist expansionism, all that is true. But this analysis was extended to far beyond the limits to which it should have gone. They even began to criticize the plantation workers for having links with India. As a result, JVP had an anti-Tamil or racist trend. At that time, not that we fully agreed with that ideology, coming from an LSSP background, I would not have agreed with that. But we were attracted by the overall direction of the JVP that we didn’t want to pick up a fight on this, that was some of us. However, there were many others in the JVP who were bought by that racist trend. That is why, even today, where we disagree very strongly with the JVP is on the analysis of the ethnic issue. I would say, that JVP has been very opportunist. This trend of opportunism has been there from the very beginning, but today it is much stronger. Because right now, the position the JVP takes with regard to the ethnic issue is totally, totally wrong. Because I think its not only racism, but also opportunism, as JVP has now become a parliamentary type of political party, wanting to get into power in collaboration with any other party possible, and in that they think a pro-Sinhala stand against any kind of devolution of power will give them more number of votes. This is a terrible mistake. Furthermore, I don’t think most people will support them on that kind of line.

Now, why did we leave the JVP? Until the experience of the insurrections, within the JVP there wasn’t much critical thinking. We were young people in larger numbers wanting to have revolution. There was no time or opportunity within the JVP for critical thinking. But, as soon as the insurrection failed and there were thousands of people inside prison, we started to think critically. Very soon, we made our critique on the positions that the JVP took, particularly on the ethnic issue and also the position that the JVP took on the Left movement. That is where we disagreed. Now, it was also the rethinking or looking back at the JVP experience that helped us to realize the following.

Firstly, JVP though it succeeded in mobilizing a number of young people in a struggle where many of them died. They failed to get involved in the class struggle. The JVP has its base among the rural youth. That was a time when the rural economy started moving into crisis. Prior to that, the state supported the rural economy. Therefore, the class conflicts within the rural sector were not really visible. And therefore it was not possible to mobilize the real rural poor, against the elitist groups in the rural areas. If you look at the political system in the country, in term of how it works. And if you look at the political history of Sri Lanka, from the British period or even earlier, we always had these rural elites. The feudals, the lords, had a dominant position and the rest of the population were under them. When the British came, they tried to break it up by abolishing the Rajakariya system, but the first time they attempted it in 1818, there was a rebellion and the British government abandoned such an attempt. Instead, what they did was absorb them into their administration, and the Rattemattayas and the rural elite, kept the people under control.

When the United National Party emerged, it emerged as the political party of the urban and rural elite, who had supported the British rule. They were in good terms and subservient to the British and in turn they were given a lot of privileges and they sustained their power and strength. Therefore the class formation of the UNP, at the very top you have the urban elite, pro-imperialist or pro-British elite, like the Senanayakes, James Pieris’, and so on. From the Tamil side there were the Ponnambalam Ramanathans, Arunachalams who were not against the British rule. So, they negotiated with the British government and inherited power. It was at that point that the UNP was formed as a political party, and hence it had that historical link, the pro-British or the pro-Western elite at the top and the rural elite in the region. And through that structure it was possible to get the support of the masses. Now, this structure was never broken. If you look at the Left movement, and particularly the early Left, the LSSP and so on were not able to break this structure. Though, they were critical of the caste system and so on, there were certain struggles, but, they were not strong enough, and there was no rural class struggle in Sri Lanka, which could separate the rural poor from the elite. And there was that dependence. Now, Bandaranaike, who was in the UNP, when he crossed over and formed his party, a major group of the rural elite moved over. If you look at the structure of the SLFP, the structures are very similar. The UNP had the urban elite, the rural elite and the rural masses. What the SLFP developed was a similar parallel structure. They also had their urban elite, rural elite and the masses under control.

In order for class struggles to advance in Sri Lanka, it was important to break up these structures. Now, the Old Left did not succeed in doing that, neither did the JVP succeed in doing that. JVP also didn’t have that struggle, but that was the time. Until then the government was able to keep the rural masses pacified. They were given a lot of concessions, benefits and subsidies and social welfare and so on. Landless were given land. Compared to India, the structures are very different; we don’t have a very powerful land owning class in Sri Lanka. We have a fairly equitable distribution of land. There is this dependency, the dependency structure is made and maintained by both political parties. When they came into power, they further used their political patronage to strengthen this structure. Therefore the structure continued and the Left failed to break this structure. Hence the Left moved from rural class struggle to urban struggle, their struggles were in the trade unions and so on. They were able to develop certain strengths through mobilizations in the urban sector and the plantation sectors. Of course the plantations, they made a huge blunder on the ethnic issue. Later therefore, the plantation workers came under the control of the Thondaman’s and so on. The Left movement gave up plantation work, when they gave up their position on the language issue. Anyway, JVP did not succeed in doing that either.

What JVP succeeded in doing was to get around or mobilize the young people who were marginalized. The young people who were not absorbed by the economy, the unemployed youth. Now, unemployed youth can become very militant, but they can’t break the class structure. Therefore the JVP’s struggle is not a real class struggle. It’s very similar to the Northern situation. In the early stages of the militancy, both in the South and the North it was the youth that fought, the youth were not included in the economic structure, so you had to rebel. If you are not involved in the economic structure, you don’t have a class struggle, you have a rebellion, no? The JVP never understood that.

This is why we thought it was absolutely necessary to go and mobilize the rural poor. That is why we got interested in working with the farmers organizations. In the early stages we were very much involved with farmers struggles. There were lots of farmers struggles; struggles around land in Monragala, struggles against water taxes. In nineteen eighty-five the government was ready to charge a water tax, they were going to charge what they called a maintenance and management fee. That was then called the water tax. The farmer organizations resisted it! We resisted it for many years. And in fact the government did not succeed in implementing that tax. Then there were the other policies of inviting foreign companies for export-oriented products like sugar, again farmers resisted. So, there were others involved.

But it was the second youth uprising in 1987, that made us realize that there was a much bigger problem. It was not enough to be involved with water struggle here and land struggle there, there was a big problem with the economy. Therefore, people had to be educated and made aware of the totality of the problem. That is how MONLAR started going into an overall analysis. All though our name is Movement on National Land and Agricultural Reform, from the beginning we have been involved in much more than land and agriculture and to address the total economic issues. And this approach was very meaningful and we were able to mobilize in a massive way for petitions and so on. 150,000 in 1994 and 300,000 later on. Now we are working on a million signature campaign. So, that is the analysis that brought us to understand this whole struggle has to be linked to the international movement against globalisation.

AK: You mentioned that the second uprising is what made you think about the overall situation. What were the particular characteristics of the second uprising, that led you to analyse the overall situation?

Sarath: There was a first uprising in 1971 when tens of thousands were killed and about twenty five thousand went to prison. In spite of that experience what was the political results? There was nothing! The Left movement did not understand this at all, they just said it was the CIA. They didn’t analyse it. That was the experience of the first insurrection. In 1971 there was this repression as a result the government that was in power since 1970 lost very badly and the UNP came to power with a big majority. It was a very strong government and they introduced the open market policies. Of course there were struggles, people resisted, people resisted when the cost of living went up. They asked for higher wages. There was a big strike in 1980; about forty thousand lost their employment. Many of the workers committed suicide and there has been emerging issues such as land policies, water taxes and so on. And then there was the Northern war.

In the late nineteen eighties came the second JVP uprising, which resulted in sixty thousand deaths. The first uprising led to ten thousand, now there was sixty thousand deaths in two years. We were in the midst of it. By that time we were in the midst of it. We had left the JVP. We were critical of the JVP. We were critical of their ethnic policy, therefore we were taking a position, when I was in the farmers movement and Devasaranayam Development Center, we took up the position that, strongly supported devolution of power and justice to the Tamil people. Because of that, JVP looked upon as enemies, as traitors. For two reasons, the fact that we had left the JVP, we were looked upon as opponents and as traitors, more strongly, because we had taken a position that was not in agreement with the JVP’s position on the ethnic issue. And that was the time that the JVP thought they could launch a revolution, by mobilizing people on the ethnic issue. The moment of revolution they thought was the coming in of the Indian Peace Keeping Forces. They thought the people would rebel. And it didn’t work, people didn’t support them. Hence the JVP started using other methods of terrorizing. Killing not only their opponents, but also anybody who disagreed with them. And they started killing people and we became victims of such killings. We lost about thirty people who were activists with us in the farmer’s movement, between 1987 to 1988. About thirty people who were working directly with us in the farmer’s movement were killed by the JVP. In some places, the state killed some of our people. So, we were victims of both.

Anyway, that experience required a much bigger analysis of what was wrong. That time, Premadasa was President, and he appointed a National Youth Commission to look into the causes of youth rebellion. Now, we were invited by the Youth Commission to make our submission. The other commission was Presidential Task Force for Land Distribution and Utilization. And we wrote a document called ‘Towards a genuine land and agricultural reform.’ Because both these insurrections pushed governments to think about land reform. The first insurrection pushed the government towards land reform in 1972 and 1975. The second insurrection, called for these commissions and we submitted our proposals where we stated that land reform alone will not solve the problem. Land reform in the seventies did not solve this crisis. We called this crisis, the crisis in the rural economies. Breakdown of rural agriculture, breakdown of the rural livelihood, increasing disparity and poverty in the rural economy. Those were the reasons, therefore we said, there is a need for a radical change to the approach of the rural economy in Sri Lanka. We made an in-depth analysis; we were critical of the green revolution, what resulted from the green revolution, the fact that poverty was not reduced, hunger was not reduced, but poverty increased. Then came the open market policies, which made it worse. So, that was our criticism. We submitted to the commissions, but we also told them, we don’t trust you, and you are not going to implement this. Instead we told them that we are going to mobilize people, we are going to educate people on this whole issue and on the crisis.

This is how it happened; we said it is now time to challenge the whole of society, the intelligentsia, the educated people, the leaderships, the religious leaderships, and the political leaderships. Because for eighteen years after the first insurrection, nobody in the country has tried to address this issue. And we have a second uprising leading to further violence. Therefore, we said, it is now up to the people to look at it and respond. That was the objective of MONLAR, to get people to respond. That was why we had a large education campaign, by 1994 we had this petition, we had conducted five to six hundred workshops and seminars through out the country. And it was very attractive, people invited us more and more to listen to this analysis and understand the situation. And that is how MONLAR developed.

AK: Regaining Sri Lanka, what is at stake? What is at stake in terms of poverty reduction, social investment and our resources?

Sarath: The most fundamental critique of Regaining Sri Lanka is that it is an attempt to further prolong and expedite the crisis. A strategy for reducing poverty is in reality, a strategy for strengthening the rich. Regaining Sri Lanka is another name for PRSP, the poverty reduction strategy. Of course, they don’t hide this fact, because in order to reduce poverty they say, you have to increase growth. The first PRSP, in fact is named that way. ‘Connecting to Growth: Sri Lanka’s Poverty Reduction Strategy.’ And the first paragraph of that document says the Government of Sri Lanka (GOSL) will try to achieve faster economic growth, as a means of reducing poverty. Therefore, the basic assumption of the PRSP is wrong, and it has been proven to be wrong. Because the PRSP in Sri Lanka is being introduced after twenty-five years of experience of trying to achieve faster growth, which they said, will trickle down and reduce poverty.

Our work shows, that poverty has not been reduced and it has not triggered growth, we used to have about 3.5% growth earlier and now we have about 5.5%. The year before last we went into negative growth. Therefore we cannot achieve this growth. The only sector that had some growth in the industry was the garments, that was for special reasons, because of this quota. Investors also came in to make use of this quota, but that is the only reason. We don’t take up this argument that economic growth didn’t work in Sri Lanka because of the war. It requires a more in-depth analysis. Why didn’t economic growth take place in Sri Lanka?

For instance, the kind of growth that was there in some countries like South Korea, Taiwan, Malaysia or the Asian Tigers was achieved at a particular time in history for particular reasons. If you read Walden Bellow, he says that was the time when the developed countries in the West supported these countries for special reasons. Firstly, they wanted to expand their labour intensive production into this region, for cheap labour. Secondly, if you look at these countries that were supported, for example Taiwan, it was supported because they wanted to counter China. South Korea was supported to contain the threat from North Korea, and Japan earlier was supported to contain Soviet expansion. So, there were special concessions given, and the policies that were adopted in these countries, again according to Walden Bello, were not mere liberalization, they were state assisted capitalism. The state played a large role.

But what Sri Lanka tried to do was different, we just liberalized everything. Therefore, from 1977 when Sri Lanka started liberalizing, it was at the wrong time in the wrong way, and we couldn’t succeed in the same way. There was no growth in agriculture, there was no foreign investment in agriculture, there was no other foreign investment, there was nothing! If you look at the cost that we have paid, we have paid a tremendous cost in terms of domestic production, and in sum we say, PRSP is the wrong strategy. This strategy has been proven to be wrong. Therefore to continue with the same strategy is foolish and not acceptable. Secondly, PRSP is not a strategy of poverty reduction, but a strategy of further subsidizing or further supporting the rich and the investors.

If you look at the concrete proposals, there are three main areas. Firstly, what do investors want? The real drafters of the PRSP are the World Bank’s advisors. It was drafted over a period of four years, that is starting in 1998, before the World Bank needed a Poverty Reduction Strategy. So what was it being drafted for? It was a strategy written to overcome the failure of the previous strategy. What were their requirements? They said, we have to provide more infrastructures. If the whole country is to become a country for investors or export, infrastructure is lacking. Therefore major proposals in the PRSP are for infrastructure development; Highways, Airports, Harbours, Telecommunications, Electricity in the regions. All that is to provide the required infrastructure, hoping that more investors will come in if the infrastructure is provided. So, what happens, in order to provide the infrastructure, we borrow money, the 4.5 Billion Dollars that we are borrowing are largely to fund this infrastructure. Who pays? The investors who are going to use this infrastructure are not required to pay for this infrastructure. They come on tax holidays. Sri Lanka has already offered up to twenty years of tax holidays. One hundred percent tax holiday going up to twenty years. One of the largest tax holiday schemes that any country has offered. Therefore, investors even if they come are not going to pay back, what we have spent on this infrastructure.

So, it is the poor, the ordinary people that will be burdened to pay back these loans. Therefore, you have to compel, push the poor to pay, which in turn are given as subsidies to the rich. That is why we named our document, ‘Compelling the Poor to Subsidize the Rich’, so it is not a strategy for poverty reduction. It’s a strategy for subsidizing and enriching the rich, by compelling the poor to take up the burden. Now, why do we say, the poor will have to pay this, because when the World Bank increases its loans, we get more and more indebted, the World Bank is not going to keep quiet, they are going to pressurize the government to pay back. How? If you are not charging the rich, you have to charge the poor. And therefore, other things like social welfare, education, health, all this selling away of assets, if you look at the PRSP proposals now, all assets in the country are being sold. The latest is the selling of Galle Face Grounds, they have sold it to private companies.

Next, they have decided to go into an agreement with the United States, they adopted this law called Tropical Forrest Conservation Act. When the U.S. was not willing to sign the Kyoto protocol to reduce Carbon Dioxide, the U.S. was blamed. So, the U.S. is going to develop a new strategy of saying, if certain countries are willing to protect their tropical forests, they are willing to reduce some of the debt. The GOSL is going to do that. What are the consequences? It is not only the case of protecting tropical forests; we would have to put these forests under the charge of the U.S. The U.S. will exploit this whole thing for their Bio-piracy! One of the most valuable resources we have in Sri Lanka is Bio-diversity including medicinal plants, which are found in our tropical forests. If we are going to hand over these resources to the US for their research, you are giving away your valuable Bio-diversity assets. That is a small part of the bargain.

We are going to privatise water. They say, water crisis. There is going to be water shortage. All nonsense! The real reason is the big water companies in the West, French, British companies, are now pushing for water privatisation around the world, so their water businesses could be expanded. It is for that reason the GOSL got into the water privatisation business. In fact this is obviously seen in the documents, in the chapter on water, they say we have to invite foreign companies. Its illogical, we need fifty billion rupees as infrastructure investment up to 2010. They say the government can’t put in the investment and they can’t even put in half, so its private investment. But if you look at the PRSP proposal, what they have borrowed or asked for water infrastructure, the total amount is more than the required investment. The required total investment is fifty billion rupees. If you add the water related projects in the PRSP it comes to 77 Billion Rupees. Which is more than the required investment. So, if the government of Sri Lanka is already borrowing that money, why do we need investors? Is it because the ordinary people are going to pay back this money? So, there is absolutely no reason why we should invite the foreign investors. Do they have better technology? No, Sri Lanka has much better technology and a much better history of ecological water management. Many of these people who are now calling themselves experts on ecological water management have learned from countries like Sri Lanka.

Another, very clear reason, they say they want to provide safe drinking water to eighty percent of the population. On the issue of water, there is absolutely no reason why we should privatise water. Now, if the requirement is that we need to provide safe drinking water, one of the first priorities should be to reduce pollution in the country. Because there is enough water, Sri Lanka is not a country that could have a water crisis in the near future. In 2025 also we will have enough water. In the water maps, Sri Lanka remains very blue. So, our priority should be to reduce pollution, but there is nothing in the water policy to reduce pollution. We are going to invite commercial agriculture. All this export oriented baby corn and other crops are going to be chemically intensive. There is a lot of potential in Sri Lanka, we have our own proposals and we have implemented some of these to do agriculture without using chemical pesticides and reduce inputs. But there is nothing in the water policy about this and hence its quite clear, the idea is not to provide people with safe drinking water. Irrigation; privatisation of irrigation is to get people out of paddy farming and out of their land for a different purpose. Therefore, what is happening is that they are going to give away a very valuable resource, water! So, companies can make their private profits.

So, water is given away, forests are given away, in relation to marine resources or fisheries resources, the policy for fisheries resources in the PRSP is to invite big foreign companies to do deep sea fishing and also export fisheries in the coastal region, like prawn farming and lagoons. So, the entire fisheries resources, or marine and inland resources, are to be given away for practically nothing. We in fact, are building some big harbours. Down South they are building some deep harbours just for big fishing vessels to come in. The PRSP contains policies about mineral resources; they are going to sell away all the mineral resources. Phosphate they attempted, people resisted, there was a long struggle against the selling of this Epawala Phosphate Resources to a U.S. company. But now the new laws in relation to mineral resources, they are going to give away all of it. So, the PRSP or Regaining Sri Lanka, firstly they are going to tax and burden the poor.

AK: Can you talk about the new land policies and land alienation. How will it impact labour?

Sarath: Now there are two new land policies. One is about land use and the other is about land ownership. In continuing the same policies, they ask the question what can we offer to the investors? We talked about infrastructure and other resources. Now, we don’t have very attractive resources, such as oil or gold, but we have land, and marine resources and bio-diversity. These are the resources to be given. Therefore the World Bank (WB) and government think, one way to invite these investors, as stated back in March 1996 in the policy recommendations of the WB, that was called Non-Plantation Sectors Policy Alternatives, written by Robert Hunt and Douglas Lister, they say as long as these people remain on the rural land, they will be cultivating paddy and other domestic food crops. They say, these are of low value and therefore there is a necessity to shift from low value to high value crops for growth. By high value crops, what they mean is export crops. So, they want to invite companies to take over land, developed and irrigated land for export production by foreign companies. To do that, they have to get the people out of the land and hence the land policy.

There is an added value, because as long as these people remain on the land because they are poor, they have no other employment, they nevertheless survive on the land, I am talking about small farmers. Even if they are running at a loss. But some how, there is a livelihood. But once you push them out of their livelihood, out of their land, they become cheap labour available for exploitation. In the PRSP they have said, they envisage a large migration of people from the rural areas to the urban areas as destitute beggars. And they will be used as cheap labour. Therefore, that would, be a factor that would help the government to have labour at a lower cost than now. And that is a requirement for the economy because we have to compete with China and other countries if we are to retain the investors.

So, that is another concern. Another aspect, which is seen as obstructing investors, is that labour is protected in Sri Lanka, in fact in1996 the WB made a policy recommendation that was called Sri Lanka in the year 2000. There they say, in Sri Lanka labour is privileged. That is labour is protected in Sri Lanka. We have since the 1930’s and 1940’s trade unions that were very active and we have certain labour laws that are relatively in favour of the workers. You can’t remove workers as you wish. There has to be a legal procedure and there must be sufficient compensation. They say we can’t have a labour market like that if we are to function on the liberalized markets of the world. Therefore, in the WB’s words create a pre-labour market, and the business community in 1998 said create a flexible labour market.

Recently, about a year ago, they introduced the Termination of Employment Act. Which gives the possibility to employers to remove labour, paying them much less compensation. What are the requirements with the new legislation? If a worker that has been working for many years needs to be removed by the employer, then the employer has to pay half a month’s salary for each year of work, if he has worked for over five years. Then for the future period, a maximum of fifteen months. All this is much less than the compensation that they used to get, if they are terminated. This is again another proposal, that is they want to remove the labour that is protected by law, and recruit new labour which can be recruited much cheaper from the rural areas.

Therefore, the three major aspects of Regaining Sri Lanka. One, create infrastructure at peoples cost. Two, provide natural resources such as land, marine, forest, mineral etc. Three, privatise the remaining services such as education. They say in education beyond tertiary education, they say Private-Public partnership should be established for higher education. Meaning private companies will be invited to sell education. Private-Public partnership means the assets of the government schools and universities, libraries etc., will be given over to private companies for their profit making. Similarly, with health, Private-Public means they will essentially become private hospitals. Now our position is that the essential services, that is basic rights such as health, water, education, food security should be provided by the government. But the government wants to give all this to the private companies. Ultimately, how is this going to reduce poverty?

AK: Can you speak a little about the process by which they came out with Regaining Sri Lanka? Was it consultative, transparent and participatory?

Sarath: Totally unparticipatory and totally undemocratic! Now, why do I say this? The strategy for poverty reduction had been worked out over a period of four years. Now, they say this has been a consultative process. If so, how is it possible? First, you have to let the people know, so that people can make their comments. Nothing of that sort happened. Nothing was revealed. The first occasion the PRSP was made known, was at the Sri Lanka development forum hosted by the WB and GOSL in June 2002. This document was discussed with the WB and there was an agreement. When that was agreed upon between the WB and GOSL, this document was not made available to anyone. Even after the WB and GOSL meeting, there was nothing that was published about what happened at that meeting. Nobody knew! Then the original PRSP was available on the internet, that was in August 2002. That was two months after the agreements were reached and it was only on the internet in English, without any printed copies. In fact on several subsequent occasions, the WB’s country director Mr. Peter Harold has openly complained, that the PRSP has not been discussed in parliament. The parliamentarians are not aware of the existence of the PRSP.

The only time it was revealed, on the 5th of July 2002 when the Prime Minister addressed the nation, he did not say anything about the PRSP, he said the country was in a crisis, there was indebtedness, the country’s debt was so much that each individual is indebted to the amount Rs. 83,000. And therefore, certain big changes have to be made in the economy. Then he said, parliament would be convened continuously over the month of August and they would approve 36 bills. People didn’t know what the bills were, but the PM said they were going to approve the 36 bills in one month. Can you think of any country, where, 36 Bills have been approved in parliament in one month? What does it mean, nobody will know anything about it. On the 5th of July he said this, the bills were not even drafted. Prof. G. L. Pieris said on the 18th of July that, some 18 Bills were drafted, the rest had not been drafted. In August they started passing these bills.

They have now legalized many of these bills. Only a few remain like water and land. But most others have been passed; the latest one was labour, the termination of employment. None of these draft legislations are made public. They are only available to the public after cabinet approval has been given. Then it is gazetted, or when Cabinet has approved it and if people want to take legal action, we have six days. Members of parliament are given the draft bill just a few hours before it’s brought up in parliament. No consultation at all. We raised these issues with WB, IMF and GOSL. We said this is not consultative and they said, no, we have consulted civil society organizations. We asked for the list of organizations with whom they consulted.

In Washington in April last year, when we were lobbying with the executive director of WB, I raised that question again. Where are the organizations that you consulted? Then they provided the list, there were some 150 names, there were 40 NGOs and all the rest were government officials, WB, IMF, private sector, the kind of names which were already supportive of the PRSP. Out of the 40 NGOs we went through it right there and said, there were more than 25 NGOs that were disagreeing. We told them, these people don’t agree! Then Peter Harold the country director said, having consulted doesn’t mean they agreed. Meaning, even the few people they have consulted have not agreed. Then, this was taken up in the Washington Conference of April 2003. And then the Tokyo Donor Conference of June 2003, where it was approved with 4.5 Billion Dollars, with the condition that it will be given in instalments of 3 months, but within the 3 months the GOSL has to prove that they have carried out the agreed conditions of legalizing it. So, every 3 months the WB or IMF will send their missions to check if the GOSL has met the conditions, therefore, there is tremendous pressure on GOSL to push through the legalizing agenda.

The moment you allow some consultation, there is resistance. Therefore, this is a totally undemocratic process. We raised the issue of language. If this is a poverty reduction strategy, the most affected are going to be the poor. And the poor must know what is going to happen, that is they are going to borrow 4.5 Billion Dollars. For what? They can’t read the internet in English! It was not available. We raised this and only about a month after the Tokyo meeting, they published it in Sinhala and Tamil. But that copy is Rs. 700 each. Then the decisions are all taken, so where is the consultation?
