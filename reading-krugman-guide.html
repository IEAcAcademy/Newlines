Mansplaining Krugman to Non-economists: "The Crisis Next Time" (Paul Krugman's Substack, 25 Oct 2026)

October 31, 2026

For decades, Krugman has made economic issues accessible to the public. This piece supports his work by explaining his ideas to readers unfamiliar with economics, focusing on the macroeconomic principles behind standard Keynesian approaches to preventing and managing recessions—and their limitations.

Krugman observes that sooner or later the US will have a serious slump. Most recessions are easy to cure: the central bank creates money and cuts interest rates. That cure fails in three situations. Stagflation: normally high unemployment comes with low inflation, but once people expect high prices, inflation stays high even in a slump, so the country suffers both at once. Liquidity trap: if the AI investment boom collapses, the Fed may need to cut rates below zero, which it cannot do. Debt crisis: investors lose faith in government bonds, rates jump, and governments slash spending. His chief concern is the liquidity trap. He warns that the next crisis, triggered by a pandemic or AI-powered hacking, could be far worse than any recent recession. So, here goes...

What is recession? What causes it?

GDP (gross domestic product) is how much a country makes every year - the total value of everything a country produces in a year: every pizza, haircut, phone, and car. Normally, GDP and the number of jobs grow every year because the population grows, and technology improves. A recession is when GDP and jobs shrink instead: shops sell less, firms cut staff, and people earn less. The key point: in most recessions, nobody has forgotten how to make things; they just aren't buying enough. A pizza shop that can bake 200 pizzas a day but only gets 120 orders lays off a cook. The cook then spends less, so other shops sell less, too. This is Keynes's central insight: one person's spending is another person's income, so a fall in spending spreads.

Ordinary recessions are usually easy to cure (in the US). Krugman is not worried about an ordinary recession but one that is "severe, protracted, or both" – one with deep impact and lasting for years.

Managing recessions: Monetary and Fiscal Policy

When spending in the economy is too weak or too strong, governments have two toolkits- monetary policy and fiscal policy. These are run by different bodies: the Federal Reserve (Fed) -the US central bank, runs monetary policy. The Fed is independent of elected officials. The House, with elected officials, runs the fiscal policy.

Monetary policy and recessions

Fed does not lend to ordinary people. It controls how much money is in the system and sets the short-term interest rate that other rates follow. The Fed has two jobs: keep inflation low and keep unemployment low. Because recessions also involve higher unemployment, these become concerns for the Fed as well. The Fed works indirectly: it changes the cost and availability of money, then relies on banks, households, and firms to choose to borrow and spend more (or less). It is fast to use since the Fed can change rates in a single meeting, but it only works if banks and people respond. (more below)

Interest rates. The price of borrowing money, as a percentage per year. Borrow $100 at 5% and you owe $105 after a year. The key point: when rates fall, borrowing gets cheaper, so families buy houses and cars on credit and firms borrow to build, and spending rises. When rates rise, spending falls, which cools the economy and slows inflation. This is the Fed's main lever. The rate it controls directly, the federal funds rate, is about 4% today. It is critical to note that changes to interest rates in the USA will impact many millions living outside the US, including those with debt in USD. For instance, when the Fed raises interest rates, the cost of the debt increases in these countries, forcing them to divert scarce resources from urgent priorities to service it.

"Printing money". This is how the Fed lowers interest rates and increases spending to prevent or manage a recession. It buys bonds from banks and pays by adding brand-new money to their accounts, money that did not exist before. Banks now have more to lend, so they offer lower interest rates to find borrowers, and lower rates encourage spending. The key point: the new money does not go to shoppers directly; it works through interest rates. That is why it can stop working (see the liquidity trap). The reverse is "tight money": the Fed raises rates to cut spending and fight inflation, as it did from 1979 to 1982.

How the swap works, step by step. The bond is the government's IOU, not the bank's; the bank owns it because it lent the government money earlier. For example,

  • A bank owns a $1 million US Treasury bond, so the government owes the bank $1 million plus interest.
  • The Fed buys back the bond and adds $1 million to the bank's account at the Fed. That money did not exist before; this is the "printing".
  • As a result, the bank now holds $1 million in cash ("reserves") instead of the bond, and the government now owes the Fed. The bank has borrowed nothing; it simply sold something it owned.
  • Then, hopefully, the bank lends that cash out. With more money looking for borrowers, banks offer lower interest rates, and cheaper loans encourage spending.

Banks sell the bond because the Fed offers a slightly better price than other buyers, and someone always takes the deal. Heavy buying pushes bond prices up, and since a bond pays a fixed amount, a higher price means a lower return, so interest rates fall. Tightening is the reverse: the Fed sells bonds cheaply enough to attract buyers, banks pay with reserves that then disappear, and rates rise. Since 2008, the Fed mostly raises rates more simply, by paying banks interest on their reserves: if the Fed pays 4%, no bank will lend for less.

Proof that this approach works

If too little spending causes a recession, and the Fed can raise spending by creating money and cutting rates, then most recessions could be made short.

The gold standard and the Great Depression. In the 1930s many countries used the gold standard: each dollar or pound could be swapped for a fixed amount of gold (in the US, $20.67 bought one ounce). So, a country could only create more money if it had more gold. Its central bank could not print money to fight a slump. During the Great Depression, the countries that left gold early, such as Britain in 1931, could expand their money supply and recovered faster. Those that held on, such as France until 1936, stayed depressed longer. Krugman uses a chart by the economic historian Barry Eichengreen to show this.

A more recent example: 1982 - From 1979 to 1982, the Fed deliberately kept money tight to fight inflation, and unemployment rose to 10.8%. In summer 1982, it decided inflation was under control and cut rates. Within months, the economy boomed, growing 8.6% in a single year from early 1983 to early 1984

Conditions that could affect the Fed’s ability to deal with recessions

So, why do some slumps last for years? Krugman's answer: there are three situations where this cure fails. Those are his three risks.

  1. Stagflation. The word joins stagnation (a weak economy with high unemployment) and inflation. The key point: the usual relationship is that high unemployment should come with low inflation. In stagflation, that relationship breaks down, and the economy suffers high inflation and high unemployment at the same time- the worst of both.

    Why does it happen? Expectations and sticky prices. Many firms change their prices and wages only about once a year, not every day. When they do, they look ahead: if they expect 10% inflation over the next year, they raise prices by 10% to avoid falling behind, and workers ask for 10% raises. The key point: expectations can make themselves come true. If everyone expects 10% inflation, everyone raises prices by 10%, and inflation is 10%, even if the economy is weak. Economists call this "entrenched" inflation. Milton Friedman and Edmund Phelps warned in the late 1960s that this would break the usual inverse relationship.

    • When it happened: the US in the 1970s and early 1980s. Inflation neared 15% in 1980 while unemployment was above 7%, and unemployment rose to 10.8% in 1982.
    • How it happens: once people expect high inflation, firms keep raising prices and workers keep demanding raises, even as sales fall and jobs disappear. A weak economy cannot stop this quickly, because the price rises are driven by expectations, not by strong demand.
    • Why the cure hurts: the only reliable cure is for the Fed to keep the economy weak, with high interest rates, for years, until people finally stop expecting high inflation. The Fed did this from 1979 to 1982. During the cure the country lives with both problems.
    • Why 2022 was different: inflation jumped to about 9% after Covid, but people still expected low inflation in the long run, so prices calmed down without a recession. Krugman calls this "immaculate disinflation".
    • Why Krugman worries now: tariffs and the war with Iran keep pushing prices up, and surveys show that people's long-run inflation expectations are creeping up. If those expectations become entrenched, the US could relive the 1970s.
  2. Liquidity trap. Remember that "printing money" works through interest rates. So, what happens when rates cannot go any lower? A negative rate means you pay the bank to keep your money. Rather than accept that, people would take out cash and keep it in a safe where it earns zero (the zero lower bound). A liquidity trap is when the Fed has cut rates all the way to zero, and the economy is still in a slump. Creating more money does little, because banks and people just hold on to it. Economists call this "pushing on a string". Once the Fed is stuck, the economy can stay depressed for years.

    After the 2008 financial crisis, the Fed cut rates to zero, yet unemployment did not return to its pre-crisis level until 2016. The key point, and Krugman's top worry: after the dot-com crash of 2000 the Fed had to cut rates by 5.5 points, from 6.5% to 1%, to bring the economy back. The AI boom is several times bigger as a share of GDP, but rates are only 4% now, so the Fed has at most 4 points to cut. If the AI boom collapses, that is probably not enough.

  3. Debt crisis: when lenders lose faith. Governments borrow by selling bonds. What happens if the buyers start to doubt they will be repaid? (See Addendum for debt and related concepts). A debt crisis is a vicious circle: fear raises interest rates, higher rates make repaying harder, and that raises fear further. Governments that respond with austerity deepen the slump. France now has a larger debt, as a share of GDP, than Spain had in 2012, big deficits, and no clear plan to fix them. Its spread over Germany has jumped, although it is still only about a third of Spain's peak. Krugman says the US debt and deficit are similar to France's.

    But the US has one big protection: it borrows in its own currency. The US borrows dollars, which its own central bank creates. Spain and France borrow euros, which they do not control. A country that borrows in its own currency can always create the money to pay, so it cannot be forced to default the way Spain nearly was. It still has risks: paying debts with new money can cause inflation, and investors can still demand higher interest. Krugman says a US debt crisis is hard to picture, "but it would be foolish to be complacent."

Fiscal policy and recessions

Fiscal stimulus (and austerity). Fiscal policy is the government's taxing and spending through its budget (recall, monetary policy is the Fed's money and interest rates). It works directly: the government spends money itself, hands money to people, or changes how much tax they pay. It does not depend on anyone choosing to borrow, but it is slow because it needs new laws, and it is shaped by politics.

Stimulus means the government spends more or taxes less, borrowing the difference, to boost demand when private spending is weak. Austerity means cutting spending or raising taxes to borrow less. The key point: when the Fed is stuck at zero, stimulus is the main tool left, which is what Keynes recommended in the 1930s. Keynesians argue that austerity during a slump makes it worse, because it removes even more demand. Krugman argues the 2009 stimulus was too small and was followed by austerity, which slowed the recovery.

Not every dollar of fiscal policy gives the same boost. Money spent directly, or given to people who spend it right away, raises demand most. Tax cuts for the wealthy raise it least, because much of that money is saved. Economists call the boost per dollar the multiplier.

What if banks and big firms use cheap money for stock buybacks instead of investing? Then share prices rise, but few new factories or jobs appear. Shareholders gain, but most are already wealthy and spend only a small share of their gains, so demand barely rises and wealth gaps widen. US firms have spent close to $1 trillion a year on buybacks recently. Critics on the left (William Lazonick) and the Austrian school both point to this. The Keynesian answer is fiscal policy, which puts money straight into people's hands and real projects.

Is fiscal policy harder for the wealthy to bend than monetary policy? Not necessarily. Krugman's case for fiscal policy is mechanical (it still works at zero interest rates), not that it is fairer. Fiscal policy can be bent too: a tax cut aimed at the rich is fiscal policy. What the money is spent on matters as much as which toolkit is used (see table below).

Two limits apply to all fiscal tools. First, they are paid for by borrowing, so they add to the debt, which matters if investors lose confidence (Krugman's Risk 3). Second, politicians decide them, so the size and mix depend on who holds power, not only on what the economy needs. Krugman argues the 2009 stimulus was too small, about a third of it was tax cuts, and the 2025 "One Big Beautiful Bill" added large deficits when the economy was already at full employment.

Conclusion

The Keynesian takeaway: in a normal slump, monetary policy is the quick fix. In a liquidity trap, it runs out, and fiscal policy has to do the work. Critics reply that fiscal policy is too slow, too political, and too costly in debt

ADDENDUM

Terminology to understand:

Bonds and yields. A bond is an IOU. You lend the government $1,000 for 10 years; it promises to pay you interest every year and then repay the $1,000. The yield is the interest rate you earn on it. The key point: if investors start to worry that a government may not repay, they will only buy its bonds if they are paid a higher yield to make up for the risk. So rising yields signal growing worry, and they also make borrowing more expensive for that government.

Demand vs. capacity (why recessions happen). Capacity is how much the economy could produce if every willing worker and every factory were busy. Demand is how much households, businesses, and government actually want to buy. The key point: in most recessions nobody has forgotten how to make things; not enough people are buying. A pizza shop that can bake 200 pizzas a day but only gets 120 orders lays off a cook. The cook then spends less, so other shops sell less too. This is Keynes's central insight: one person's spending is another person's income, so a fall in spending spreads.

Federal Reserve (Fed, Central Bank). The Federal Reserve is the US government's bank. It does not lend to ordinary people. It controls the money supply and sets the short-term interest rate that other rates follow. It has two jobs: keep inflation low (about 2%) and keep unemployment low. The key point: these two jobs often pull in opposite directions.

GDP (output). The total value of all the goods and services a country produces in a year: every haircut, phone, meal, and car. When the news says "the economy grew 2%", it means that GDP grew 2%. Krugman uses GDP as a yardstick: AI investment is 2.4% to 3.2% of GDP each year, meaning that share of everything the US produces goes into building AI data centres.

Government debt

Deficit and debt. The deficit is how much more a government spends than it collects in taxes in one year. The debt is the total of all past deficits still owed. Think of a person who spends $50 more than they earn this month (the deficit) and now has a $2,000 credit card balance (the debt). Both are usually measured as a percentage of GDP, so that countries of different sizes can be compared. The key point: a large deficit year after year makes the debt keep growing, and Krugman says the US and France are both in that position with no political plan to stop it.

Spread and investor confidence. The spread is the extra interest a country pays on its bonds compared with a country seen as very safe, which in Europe is Germany. If Germany pays 2.5% and France pays 3.3%, the spread is 0.8 points. The key point: the spread measures fear. In 2012 investors feared Spain could not repay, and its spread rose above 6 points. Borrowing became very expensive; the government made deep spending cuts to reassure investors, and unemployment passed 26%. It is a vicious circle: fear raises interest rates, higher rates make repaying harder, and that raises fear further. The panic eased when the head of the European Central Bank, Mario Draghi, promised to do "whatever it takes" to support the euro.

Borrowing in your own currency. The US borrows in dollars, which its own central bank creates. Spain and France borrow in euros, which the European Central Bank controls, not them. The key point: a country that borrows in its own currency can always create the money to pay, so it cannot be forced to default the way Spain nearly was. It still faces risks: paying debts with newly created money can cause inflation, and investors can still demand higher interest. That is why Krugman says a US debt crisis is hard to picture, but not impossible.

Gold standard. A system, common until the 1930s, in which each unit of a currency could be swapped for a fixed amount of gold (in the US, $20.67 bought one ounce). The key point: a country could only create more money if it had more gold to back it, so its central bank could not "print money" to fight a slump. In the Great Depression, countries that left gold early, such as Britain in 1931, could expand their money supply and recover faster than those that held on, such as France until 1936. [This is Krugman's Chart 1, and his evidence that the money cure works.]

Inflation. The rate at which prices rise over a year. With 3% inflation, a basket of goods that cost $100 last year costs $103 now, so each dollar buys less. The key point: a little inflation, about 2% a year, is normal and is what the Fed aims for. High inflation, like the 10% or more of around 1980, is harmful because it eats into savings and wages and makes planning hard. Inflation rises for two reasons: people want to buy more than the economy can produce, or costs jump (for example, tariffs, or oil prices after a war).

Unemployment. The share of people who want a job and are looking for one but cannot find it. Retirees and students who are not looking do not count. It is the most painful cost of a recession: US unemployment reached 10.8% in late 1982, and in Spain it passed 26% in 2013, more than one worker in four.

Phillips Curve-Inflation and Unemployment. Economist Bill Phillips spotted a pattern in 1958 in British data on wages and unemployment - normally, the two move in opposite directions: when one is high, the other is low. Why does it happen? When spending is strong, firms hire, so unemployment falls; with workers scarce, firms must offer higher wages, and with plentiful customers, they can raise prices, so inflation rises. When spending is weak, firms lay people off, so unemployment rises; with few customers, they hold prices down, so inflation falls. As noted earlier, when the Fed raises interest rates, spending is expected to fall, and vice versa. The key point: this gave governments a menu. They could accept a bit more inflation to get fewer people out of work, or more unemployment to get lower inflation, but not both low at once. This is the rule that stagflation breaks.
