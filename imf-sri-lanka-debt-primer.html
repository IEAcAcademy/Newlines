IMF and Sri Lanka Debt Crisis - A Primer

November 15, 2026

1. The Context

By 1944, the Allied governments were already planning the peace. Their fundamental lesson from the 1920s and 1930s was economic. In the 1920s and 1930s, countries had adopted 'beggar-thy-neighbour' policies: competitive currency depreciations and higher tariffs, such as the US Smoot-Hawley Tariff Act, which protected US farmers from imports [1]. US President Roosevelt and his advisers, notably Secretary of State Cordell Hull, argued that the 1930s showed that high tariffs, discriminatory trading blocs and manipulated currencies left everyone worse off, and that the resulting hard times fed the nationalism that led to the war [2]. Planning for the IMF's charter began in 1941 and ended at the Bretton Woods Conference [3]. Meeting before the war ended meant that the rules could be agreed while Allied cooperation was strong and before postwar pressures set in..

Protectionism involves using tariffs, import quotas and similar barriers to shield domestic producers from foreign competition and, Autarky called for national self-sufficiency, in the extreme case almost no trade. When one country raised barriers or devalued its currency to sell more abroad, its partners retaliated, world trade shrank, and unemployment rose everywhere- i.e., it triggered a chain reaction [2]. Under the interwar gold standard, the problem was amplified. A country with a trade deficit either raised tariffs or cut the money supply to prevent its gold reserves from being drained. Tightening the money supply pushes down prices (deflation) and employment [2]. Governments therefore faced a choice between deflation at home and restrictions on imports. Recent research finds that the beggar-thy-neighbour effects of 1930s devaluations were limited and temporary, and that rising tariffs, import quotas and bilateral agreements did most of the damage to trade [4]. The 1944 planners nonetheless believed the 1930s had shown the danger.

Rationale

The IMF could not itself lower tariffs; that was left to a separate trade agreement, the GATT, negotiated alongside Bretton Woods but outside it [5]. Its contribution was to remove the monetary reasons for trade restrictions. First, members would keep fixed but adjustable exchange rates, supervised by the Fund, so that currencies were not devalued competitively [6]. Second, the Fund would lend foreign exchange for short periods to a member to make their payments (with a payments deficit) [2]. This allowed the member to keep its exchange rate and its imports going while it adjusted, instead of devaluing its currency or restricting imports at once [6]. The Articles of Agreement state this aim: to give members the chance to correct balance-of-payments maladjustments 'without resorting to measures destructive of national or international prosperity' [6]. 'Multilateral' trade means trade among many countries on open terms, as opposed to bilateral deals in which each pair of countries balances its own trade. It requires currencies that can be freely exchanged (see Annex A).

The Origins

The IMF and the World Bank were designed at the United Nations Monetary and Financial Conference at Bretton Woods, New Hampshire, in July 1944, attended by delegates from 44 countries [7, 8]. Records point to the IMF's formal establishment as 27 December 1945, with 29 countries as signatories of its Articles of Agreement [9]. John Maynard Keynes (economist) led the British delegation, and Harry Dexter White was the chief drafter for the United States [8, 3]. Keynes proposed an international clearing union; White proposed a stabilisation fund built on member quotas [8, 3]. The two plans were merged after about two years of negotiation, and the US view prevailed on most points: White's IMF was to be smaller than Keynes's and was to allocate its scarce resources selectively rather than freely on demand [8, 10].

Debates on the vision and functions of the Bretton Woods Institutions

The central debate was on conditionality. White's April 1942 plan proposed that the Fund assess a country's policies and require changes before approving credit. Keynes argued for more automatic access with fewer strings [3]. The same tension reappears in debates about IMF programmes today (Section 3 and Annex C). The Fund's role was to promote monetary cooperation, supervise the exchange-rate system and lend short term to members in payment difficulty [2]. The World Bank was to finance reconstruction of war-damaged economies and, later, development [7, 8]. In brief, the Fund deals with the balance of payments, and the Bank with long-term investment. (Annex D provides details on these debates)

2. Governance: Decision-making and allocations

The Board of Governors, with one governor and one alternate from each member, is the IMF's highest body. It delegates day-to-day decisions to the Executive Board [11, 12].

Quotas, votes and SDRs: the principle

The IMF works like a cooperative in which members' voting power depends on their financial stake. Each member is assigned a quota, a sum of money it contributes. The quota does three jobs: it sets the member's financial commitment, helps determine how much it can borrow, and sets its voting power and its share of any SDR allocation [13, 14]. Quotas are meant to reflect a country's weight in the world economy and are reviewed at least every five years [15]. (See the IMF quota page [11].)

A vote has two parts. Every member receives the same 250 'basic votes', which gives small countries a floor of representation, and then one additional vote for each SDR 100,000 of its quota [13, 15]. Because the second part dominates, large economies hold most of the votes. The SDR (Special Drawing Right) is an international reserve asset the IMF created in 1969. It is used here as the Fund's unit of account, so quotas are stated in SDRs [16]. When the IMF creates new SDRs, it allocates them to members in proportion to their quotas [16]. A larger quota therefore means a bigger share of new SDRs and more votes. Whether the system is fair is contested: it matches influence to financial contribution, but gives the countries with the least economic weight, including many borrowers, the smallest say. Emerging and developing economies hold about 40% of the votes against about 60% of world GDP [15].

The veto power of the US

Most decisions need a simple majority, but amending the Articles of Agreement and changing quotas need 85% of the votes [14]. Till recently, the United States held over 15% of the votes and so had the monopoly on the power to block structural changes; this is the 'US veto' [14, 17]. The veto power can be exercised only over a structural change, not over every loan. The 2023 reforms - agreement to raise quotas by 50% needed 85% of votes [13, 17]. If the reform to raise the quotas is passed, the existing formula will realign the quotas, resulting in the US losing its blocking position. This requires US congressional approval, and there has not been a vote on this measure, thus far [15].

Europe's influence rests on a 1944 'gentleman's agreement': an American head the World Bank, and a European leads the IMF [18]. EU members together hold an estimated 25.3% of the quota, which critics say over-represents Europe [13].

Lending decisions.

The IMF does not choose borrowers by merit or politics in a formal sense. A member in actual or potential need of external financing asks for a programme; staff negotiate the policies and amount with the government; and the Executive Board votes on the package [19, 20]. The amount is set as a share of the country's quota: under the Extended Fund Facility, 'normal access' is 200% of quota in any 12 months and 600% cumulatively, with larger 'exceptional access' decided case by case [19]. This is why large economies can borrow larger sums. The Fund also limits risk: it expects the country to be able to repay and, in most cases, its debt to be sustainable, and it lends in tranches tied to reviews [19, 21].

These rules can be selectively applied. The loans to Ukraine are a case in point. The IMF's earlier rule was that a programme required the country's payments problem to be resolvable, with medium-term external viability, and the country to be able to repay. Neither could be assumed during a war [22]. After first giving emergency financing through the Rapid Financing Instrument, the Board approved a four-year EFF of SDR 11.6 billion on 31 March 2023 [22]. To make this possible, the IMF changed its financing-assurances policy. It accepted upfront creditor commitments to debt relief with a contingent element and used 'capacity-to-repay' assurances from key creditors to protect the Fund's resources; 13 countries gave such assurances [22]. The Fund's 'lending into arrears' policy also was invoked to lend while Ukraine was in arrears on some private debts, citing prompt Fund support was essential and Ukraine was making a good-faith effort to agree with creditors [23]. The programme was backed by a large external financing envelope, including roughly USD 50 billion in G7 loans backed by immobilised Russian assets [23].

3. Evolution of the Lending Portfolio

The early decades

Under the original system (about 1945 to 1971), the Fund mainly supervised exchange rates and made short-term loans to help members meet temporary payment deficits. The Stand-By Arrangement was created in 1952 [24]. A secondary source suggests the main users in the 1950s and 1960s were advanced economies [25]. The Fund was created in part to finance the countries facing a balance of payment crisis to address the external gap while the country adjusts so that adjustment can be orderly rather than abrupt (Annex A).

Radical shift in 1971

In August 1971, President Nixon unilaterally suspended the convertibility of the dollar into gold [26]. The 1944 system had pegged other currencies to the dollar, and the dollar to gold; once the link broke, the system of fixed rates collapsed by 1973 [27]. The IMF's original task of helping countries defend a peg lost its main purpose, but payment problems did not disappear. Instead, the 1970s brought major shocks: sharp oil price rises, and easy access to private capital markets [28]. Oil-importing developing countries suddenly needed large amounts of foreign currency for medium-term adjustment, which short-term loans could not provide. The Fund responded with an oil facility, the Extended Fund Facility (1974), and a Trust Fund financed by gold sales for low-income countries [24, 29]. In short, lending replaced exchange-rate policing as the Fund's central activity, mainly for developing countries.

Debt crisis and surveillance

Many developing countries borrowed heavily from commercial banks in the 1970s and early 1980s. The spike in US interest rates to control its double-digit inflation resulted in a large-scale debt crisis around the world in 1982 [30]. The IMF became crisis manager [26, 28]. Its approach was case-by-case: provide a limited amount of its own money, require policy adjustment, and 'catalyse' further financing from banks and other creditors. Fund approval became a precondition for creditors' agreements [31]. Concessional facilities for poorer countries followed: the Structural Adjustment Facility (1986) and Enhanced SAF (1987) [24]. The Fund's 'surveillance' role grew in this period [27]. Surveillance means regularly monitoring members' economic and exchange-rate policies and advising them, in order to detect problems early (general background; see Annex A).

Terms of lending

Loans carry conditionality: the borrower commits to policies prescribed by the IMF, and money is released in installments only if targets are met. Early conditions were macroeconomic: limits on credit and budget deficits and reserve floors. From the 1980s, they expanded to structural reforms such as tax, subsidy and state-enterprise changes [24, 29]. In 2002, the IMF issued guidelines stressing country ownership and limiting conditions to those critical to a programme's goals; after the 2008 crisis, credit lines were increased and structural benchmarks were dropped as conditions for disbursement [29, 24]. How these conditions came to be associated with the 'Washington Consensus', and why critics object, is presented in Annex C. The IMF's own assessments have recognized the weaknesses of these conditionalities, but the policy prescriptions continue. Annex C discusses conditionality and its criticisms.

4. Debt Relief: Processes and Current Practice

Two points of clarification. First, ordinary IMF loans are not debt relief; they must be repaid. Second, the IMF itself rarely 'forgives' debt. Relief typically comes from other creditors, and the IMF sets the conditions under which that happens by assessing whether a country's debt is sustainable. The IMF's own main relief schemes were for the poorest countries, namely the Heavily Indebted Poor Countries (HIPC) Initiative, launched in 1996, and the 2005 Multilateral Debt Relief Initiative (MDRI), which canceled eligible debts owed to the IMF, World Bank and African Development Fund [32, 33].

Example 1: The HIPC route (low-income countries)

  1. Eligibility: the country must be eligible for concessional World Bank and IMF lending, face an unsustainable debt burden even after traditional relief, and have a record of reform [32, 33].
  2. Preparation: it prepares a Poverty Reduction Strategy Paper, builds a track record under an IMF/World Bank-supported programme, and clears arrears to the main multilateral lenders [32, 34].
  3. Debt sustainability analysis: IMF and World Bank staff, with the authorities, analyse each loan to see whether traditional relief would be enough [34].
  4. Decision point: the two Executive Boards decide that the country qualifies; creditors commit to reduce debt to a sustainable level, and interim relief begins [32, 34].
  5. Interim period: the country continues reforms under concessional lending [34].
  6. Completion point: after meeting agreed triggers and sustaining macroeconomic stability, the committed relief becomes irrevocable [32, 34].

Example 2: IMF Rescue #17 of Sri Lanka (2022-23)

Sri Lanka was not a HIPC country. It received no 'forgiveness' from the IMF; it restructured its debts with its creditors inside an IMF-supported programme, the country's first sovereign debt restructuring [20]. The sequence was as follows.

  • Request. Sri Lanka formally asked for IMF support under the EFF in March 2022 and announced a moratorium on foreign debt payments in April 2022, while hiring financial and legal advisers [20, 21].
  • Debt assessed as unsustainable. In June 2022, IMF staff stated that public debt was unsustainable, so Board approval would need adequate assurances from creditors that sustainability would be restored [35]. The Fund does not lend to a country whose debt is judged unsustainable without an upfront debt treatment [36].
  • Staff-level agreement (1 September 2022). Staff agreed with the authorities on a 48-month EFF of about SDR 2.2 billion (USD 2.9 billion). The goals were macroeconomic stability, debt sustainability, protection of the vulnerable, and reforms against corruption. The agreement was subject to prior actions, financing assurances from official creditors, and good-faith efforts to reach an agreement with private creditors [37]. Key policies included raising revenue, with a primary surplus target of 2.3% of GDP by 2025, and a stronger central bank [37].
  • Financing assurances. 'Financing assurances' are written commitments from the main bilateral creditors to grant debt relief consistent with the programme's targets. They were required before the IMF could move ahead [20, 36]. With private creditors, a 'good faith' effort means early dialogue, timely information-sharing and giving creditors input into the restructuring framework [36]. IMF management provided 'good offices' to help obtain assurances [20].
  • Board approval (20 March 2023), nearly seven months after the staff-level agreement. The approved amount was SDR 2.286 billion (395% of quota, about USD 3 billion) [20, 38]. The programme assumed about USD 17 billion in debt service reduction over 2023-27 and set targets such as public debt below 95% of GDP by 2032 [21].
  • Negotiation and reviews. After approval, the negotiations focused on treatment terms consistent with the debt targets [20]. Each semi-annual review includes a Financing Assurances Review to confirm that restructuring is progressing in line with those targets [39].

The Sri Lanka EFF is a 48-month arrangement. By 27 May 2026, the Board had completed the combined Fifth and Sixth Reviews, bringing total purchases to SDR 1.778 billion (about USD 2.4 billion) of the SDR 2.286 billion approved [38]. The schedule showed the Seventh Review on or after 1 October 2026 and the Eighth on or after 1 March 2027 [38]. The IMF said that, after temporary relief spending following Cyclone Ditwah, Sri Lanka should return to the 2.3% of GDP primary balance target by 2027, with continued price stability, greater exchange-rate flexibility and structural reforms [38]. It appears that no public decision on any extension or successor arrangement has taken place.

EFFs typically last three years and may run to four [19], so continued IMF support after the Eighth Review would require a new or modified Board decision. It can be inferred that factors affecting that decision would include delivery of review conditions, the progress of creditor agreements, and the medium-term debt targets noted above [21, 38].

Eligibility for the EFF: any member facing actual or potential external financing needs. It addresses serious medium-term payment problems rooted in structural weaknesses, and is mostly used by advanced and emerging economies, though low-income countries sometimes combine it with the Extended Credit Facility. Repayment is over 4½ to 10 years, and EFFs are generally not precautionary [19].

Annex A. Technical Terms

Balance of payments (BoP). The record of a country's transactions with the rest of the world: [current account + (capital + financial) accounts]: exports and imports of goods and services, income, transfers (e.g. Aid, remittances), and capital flows (long-term investments). A BoP problem arises when a country cannot finance its external payments from export earnings and borrowing, so its foreign-exchange reserves run low. It mattered in 1944 because under the gold standard, such countries had to deflate and cut imports [2], and because without reserves, they might devalue or restrict imports, spreading the damage to partners [1]. The IMF was created in part so that such countries could borrow to adjust gradually [2, 6].

Currency convertibility. The ability to exchange one currency for others, or for gold, at an official rate without restriction. The Bretton Woods system required countries to guarantee convertibility of their currencies into US dollars, with the dollar convertible to gold for foreign governments and central banks [9]. Free convertibility was seen as key to multilateral trade because a seller can be paid in any currency and spend it anywhere.

Debt sustainability. A judgement, made by IMF staff, that a country can meet its debt obligations under realistic policies without exceptional relief.

Financing assurances. Commitments by creditors, before IMF Board approval, that they will provide debt relief or consent to IMF lending so that the programme is financed and debt becomes sustainable [36].

IMF Conditionality. Policy commitments attached to IMF lending, with funds released in tranches after performance criteria and structural benchmarks are met (Section 3).

IMF Surveillance. Monitoring and advising on members' economic and exchange-rate policies to detect risks early (general background).

Quota (IMF General Assembly Members). A member's assigned financial contribution to the IMF, which also sets its borrowing limits, its voting power and its SDR share (Section 2).

Special Drawing Rights (SDR). An international reserve asset created by the IMF in 1969 to supplement members' reserves, allocated in proportion to quotas [16]. It is also the IMF's unit of account. Its value is based on a basket of major currencies.

Short-term liquidity. Quickly available foreign exchange, or reserves to meet immediate payments. The IMF's original role was to provide this so that a temporary shortfall did not force harsh adjustment [2]. It differs from solvency, where a country's debt is too large to be repaid at all; solvency problems call for restructuring.

Annex B. How Many Countries Received Debt Relief?

HIPC and MDRI: the World Bank reports that the two programmes relieved 37 participating countries of more than USD 100 billion [33]. The IMF's fact sheet (February 2023) said that 36 of the 39 eligible countries had reached the completion point, and the World Bank notes that Somalia completed in December 2023 [32, 33]. Banca d'Italia, writing in 2022, reported Somalia and Sudan in the interim phase between decision and completion point [40].

Repeated relief: An IMF working paper counted over 600 sovereign debt restructurings in 95 countries from 1950 to 2010, and found that some governments have had more than a dozen [41]. The Banca d'Italia paper describes a 'too little, too late' pattern in which several interim restructurings occur before a crisis ends [40]. A CEPR column likens this to 'serial default' [42]. These figures cover all sovereign restructurings, not only IMF-linked relief, and end in 2010; a count of recent repeaters would need a more recent dataset.

Annex C. Conditionality and its critics

Before the 1980s, IMF loan conditions were mostly limited to short-term macroeconomic targets (such as money supply, exchange rates, and budget deficits). During the 1980s—particularly with the launch of the Structural Adjustment Facility (SAF) in 1986 and the Enhanced Structural Adjustment Facility (ESAF) in 1987—the IMF dramatically expanded its conditions to mandate deep microeconomic and institutional changes, including privatization, deregulation, and trade liberalization. [48, 49, 52, 53, 54] The term 'Washington Consensus' was coined in 1989 by economist John Williamson for a set of policy recommendations (general background).

The reasoning was that debt crises were caused by fiscal indiscipline and state intervention, so the cure was stabilisation plus market reforms.

Mainstream critics. Joseph Stiglitz, a Nobel laureate and former World Bank chief economist, argues that IMF treatment was too simple and uniform ('one size fits all'): stabilise, liberalise and privatise, without attention to sequencing or side effects [44, 45]. Dani Rodrik of Harvard argues there is no convincing evidence that openness to trade and capital flows systematically raises growth, and that developing countries should engage with the world economy on their own terms [46]. Even Williamson is reported as accepting that the 1990 reforms are no longer adequate [46]. A widely cited review concluded that the Washington Consensus policies were 'neither necessary nor sufficient' for growth, though each made sense for some countries at some times [46]. Defenders respond that programmes were negotiated, that conditions protect the Fund's lending resources, and that the alternative was default without finance; evidence on effectiveness is mixed [25]. Suggested primary readings: Stiglitz, Globalization and Its Discontents (2002), and Rodrik, 'Goodbye Washington Consensus, Hello Washington Confusion?' [44].

The IMF's own staff and its Independent Evaluation Office (IEO) have published several landmark internal reviews and evaluations critiquing the rapid expansion of "structural conditionality" that began in the 1980s. [51, 55]

Key IMF Internal and Evaluation Reports to Reference

  • The ESAF at Ten Years: Economic Adjustment and Reform in Low-Income Countries (IMF Occasional Paper No. 156, 1997). Prepared by IMF staff to review the 1986–1995 period, this internal study acknowledged major shortcomings in the structural adjustment programs introduced in the late 1980s. It found high rates of program interruption, weak economic growth outcomes compared to projections, and a failure to protect vulnerable populations from short-term austerity shocks. [56, 48, 57]
  • External Evaluation of the ESAF: Report by a Group of Independent Experts (IMF, 1998). Commissioned directly by the IMF Executive Board (led by Kwesi Botchwey) alongside the 1997 internal staff review, this report offered a sharp critique of 1980s-era structural adjustment programs. It concluded that heavy-handed, externally imposed conditions undermined "country ownership," sequenced reforms poorly, and often hurt the poor through cuts to social services. [57, 48]
  • Conditionality in Fund-Supported Programs—Overview (IMF Policy Development and Review Department, 2001). This major internal staff review examined the explosion of structural conditions from the mid-1980s through the 1990s. It admitted that structural conditionality had grown overly detailed, strayed outside the IMF's core areas of expertise, and overwhelmed the administrative capacity of borrowing governments, directly prompting the IMF to issue its 2002 Guidelines on Conditionality calling for "parsimony". [58, 49, 51]
  • Evaluation of Prolonged Use of IMF Resources (IEO, 2002). Published by the IMF's Independent Evaluation Office (established in 2001), this inaugural report analyzed how countries entering IMF structural programs in the 1980s became trapped in decades-long cycles of repeated borrowing. It faulted the IMF for overly optimistic growth forecasts and excessive, poorly prioritized structural conditions that failed to resolve underlying economic weaknesses. [50, 53, 51]
  • Structural Conditionality in IMF-Supported Programs (IEO, 2007). This evaluation assessed whether the IMF had fixed the intrusive conditionality model rooted in the 1980s. It famously concluded that structural conditions remained "very detailed, not obviously critical, and often felt to be intrusive," noting that compliance with structural conditions was only around 50% (compared to 85% for traditional macroeconomic targets) and had little correlation with lasting structural reform. [51]

Core Criticisms Highlighted in These Reports

  • Mission creep and loss of focus. The IMF moved away from John Maynard Keynes's and Harry Dexter White's original vision of short-term balance-of-payments support into long-term development engineering, often duplicating World Bank mandates.
  • Erosion of Country Ownership. Internal reviews repeatedly found that when reforms were dictated from Washington rather than designed domestically, governments either abandoned the programs or implemented changes only on paper.
  • Proliferation of Conditions. Academic and archival datasets tracking IMF agreements show that the average number of binding conditions per program jumped from roughly 4–7 in the 1970s to over 12 in the late 1980s—and regularly exceeded 17 structural conditions per year by the 1990s. [54, 49, 59, 55, 60, 51].

Annex D. Competing formative visions for IMF

At the 1944 Bretton Woods Conference, Harry Dexter White championed a US-backed stabilization fund anchored to the US dollar and gold, while John Maynard Keynes proposed a global central bank with its own supranational currency designed to penalize both debtor and creditor nations. Their competing blueprints reflected the economic and geopolitical interests of their respective countries: the United States held most of the world's gold and ran massive trade surpluses, whereas Britain was deeply in debt from World War II and faced severe trade deficits.

Their visions:

  • White's Creditor-Focused Approach. The US viewed international finance through the lens of a cautious lender and opted for a smaller, controlled pool of money where voting power matched financial contributions, ensuring that it would benefit from rebuilding the world economy.
  • Keynes's Systemic Approach. Drawing on the precarious financial position of European countries, Keynes worried that forcing indebted nations into strict austerity would trigger another global depression. His International Clearing Union was designed to recycle trade surpluses back into the global economy by penalizing countries (like the US) that hoarded gold and ran persistent trade surpluses.

The Outcome at Bretton Woods

Because the United States held overwhelming economic and political leverage at the end of World War II, the final Bretton Woods Agreement largely followed White's blueprint.

  • Creation of the IMF and World Bank. Instead of Keynes's Clearing Union, delegates established the International Monetary Fund (IMF) and the International Bank for Reconstruction and Development (World Bank), both headquartered in Washington, D.C.
  • The rise of the US dollar. Keynes's bancor was rejected, cementing the US dollar as the world's primary reserve currency.
  • Keynes's Concessions. Keynes successfully negotiated slightly larger lending quotas and a "scarce currency" clause meant to modestly pressure persistent surplus nations, though that clause was never enforced in practice.
