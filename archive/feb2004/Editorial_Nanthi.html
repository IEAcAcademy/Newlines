Editorial Comments · February 2004

Sri Lanka Elections: Peace as if People Mattered

Nanthikesan

Elite politics has dragged us all into its destructive wake and left an uninvited guest at our doorstep – April elections! The political ‘leaders’ shall party away with their predictable moves – serving neo-liberal wine in populist bottles – we are allowed to decide how much wine we can stomach, and, of course, the color of the bottle. We have little choice in the face of institutionalized election violence. This is the picture of the South on a broad canvas. As for the North and East, well, let’s change the subject quickly, or else we’ll all end up discussing the weather with one voice.

This is not to say that the outcomes of the elections are of no consequence – the amount of neo-liberal ‘wine’ we are forced to consume affects many. Without detracting from the issues of bread and pol sambol, it is clear that this election is also about our on-off peace process. Donors and political analysts have all reminded us, quite justifiably, that elections are not good news for the peace process. While agreeing with them, I would like to add to the voices of many fellow voters that we also need to worry about what kind of peace we are after, as much as we worry about the peace process. The two seem to be separated in practice.

Indeed, a closer look at the choices emanating from the party platforms shows that while there may be different takes on the peace processes, in terms of visions of peace, it is clear that the southern voter has not much to choose from. First, let me list the three key choices we are presented with:

  • UNF platform wants the peace process to continue regardless of the cost to the communities in the North and East.

  • The PA/JVP platform is increasingly aligned with the view that the peace process is a threat to Sinhala-Buddhist hegemony in a unitary Sri Lanka

  • The Left supports the peace process, but views acceding to LTTE demands as the only option available to defend the gains of Tamil self-determination struggle that have been accomplished.

The UNP supports the current peace process because it has realized that war is inimical to business interests in the South - to most segments of the business community (perhaps with the exception of those benefiting from the war economy). The absence of war removes an obstacle to harnessing the gains from the past three decades of neoliberal policies. This is a dramatic shift from the policies pursued by JR and the UNP until the turn of the century – which had twin priorities: ‘putting Tamils in their proper place’ and pursuing neo-liberal growth.

This new UNP vision argues for the continuation of the peace process at any cost… (read at any cost to the North and East)… insofar as that translates to business as usual in the South. This vision could not care less about those living in the North and East – Sinhalese, Tamils, and Muslims alike. It is linked to a minimalist notion of peace as the absence of war. Its vision for peace is not one that leads to a future for those living in the North and East free from fear, oppression, and dehumanizing disempowerment in every walk of life.

The UNF's support for the peace process is predicated on a brand of chauvinism, as many observers have noted, evident in its willingness to subjugate the interests of the Tamils and Muslims in the North and East to its own. Hence, its denial of ongoing child recruitment, extortion, systematic intimidation, and political murders committed by the LTTE, as well as the rising tensions between the Muslims and Tamils in the North and East. This brand of chauvinism is informed by the cost of war to the neo-liberal economic initiatives and is a recent entry into mainstream politics in the South.

This new brand of chauvinism shows continuities with the elitism of leaders linked to familiar populist chauvinism, in their willingness to sacrifice a few poor Sinhalese for the ‘cause’– For instance, the latter would settle Sinhalese youth in irrigation schemes in the East - ostensibly to provide them with much-needed economic opportunities. In doing so, the ruling elite knowingly exposed them to the risks of an ethnically charged and - what has now become - violent environment. These settlements also altered the demography of the East and enabled gerrymandering for a more entrenched Sinhala majority in the parliament – They became a major bone of contention in the struggle for self-determination by the Tamils.

The current PA/JVP alliance shares more than unease about the concessions extended by the UNF government to the LTTE. JVP and significant segments of PA are aligned with the familiar brand of chauvinism that views the peace process as a threat to the Sinhala Buddhist hegemony in a unitary Sri Lanka. At present, this vision is a continuation of the chauvinist agenda that has habitually scuttled initiatives to bring political resolution to minority concerns - from the Kelani march of JR in the ‘30s, to the Banda-Chelva pact, to the forces that stalled the peace drive in the 90’s. Fortunately, this familiar Sinhala chauvinism, shared by UNP and SLFP ever since they entered parliamentary politics, is no longer a sure ticket to election victories. However, it remains an ideological force to reckon with.

The Left and the liberals seem to share a vision that the peace process must be protected and that the rights of Tamils and Muslims must be recognized as a principle. They correctly see the peace process as the necessary first step toward bringing peace with justice. However, this vision fails to go beyond supporting the abstract idea of Tamil self-determination. They have become intellectually paralyzed when it comes to dealing with ground realities, which show that the foremost enemies of Tamil self-determination are not only the chauvinist forces in the South and the state but also those who are fighting for this self-determination. Often, the Left and the liberals defend their position by asserting that Tamils should be allowed to determine their own fate. The question then is, in the absence of democratic space, how they can achieve this. Equally problematic is how this hands-off approach is going to help the Muslims and the Sinhalese in the North and East.

This is not to undermine the courageous role the Left and the liberals in the South played in championing minority rights. However, it is time for them to extend their visionary thinking and move beyond the rhetoric of self-determination. The priority is to foster conditions that will expand the democratic space being denied to those in the North and East.

The absence of war could enable the reopening of some democratic spaces that were closed during the war. But this is by no means an automatic process. Thus, support for the peace process needs to navigate the tension between keeping the LTTE at the negotiating table and creating conditions that foster sustainable peace. The latter requires processes to create and expand democratic space in the North and East – such as monitoring human rights violations and incorporating human rights as an aspect of peace negotiations.

However, the Left and the liberals have been reluctantly reacting to the revelations – thanks to the tireless efforts of isolated individuals and CSOs - that paint a picture of deeply troubling conditions in the North and East. This information seems to have no bearing on their position in acceding to LTTE demands as the only option available to defend the gains that the Tamil self-determination struggle has accomplished – save for some slap-on-the-wrist comments/condemnations. Curiously, the consequence of this vision is not dissimilar to that of the UNP Vision, which basically gives a free hand to the LTTE in the North and East. For a consequentialist, this vision is not different from an LTTE point of view that sees itself as the sole representative of the Tamil people.

While there is a diversity of views on the peace process, none of the visions of peace stand for peace with dignity and justice for the peoples of the North and East. This neglect, unfortunately, is not new. We seem to be relying on the same neglect that fueled the crisis to end it!

History has shown how costly this neglect can be for the country. The forthcoming election is an opportunity and a responsibility for all Southern parties to address this void. Peoples of the North and South will not be able to express their views publicly in this election – which makes it all the more important for the rest of the country to heed their call and bring about peace with justice.

This is a platform for addressing the peace process in ways that go beyond debates about the best possible constitutions and administrative boundaries. The peace process is not about peace in the distant future, divorced from current realities. Peace that emerges from current processes, if it does, will be marked by the limitations (and strengths) of those practices – a weak emphasis on democratic accountability now cannot be expected to bring about just peace down the road.

This call should not be read as insisting on perfectly accountable structures in place right at the beginning. We do realize the inevitable need for a process that takes incremental steps when negotiating such difficult terrain as ours. However, each step cannot be blind to the larger vision of bringing about just peace. For instance, right at the onset of the process, we need to institute checks such as human rights monitoring – such a move is one of the vital steps necessary to bring about a culture of democratic accountability.

This election offers an opportunity to speak of the processes and practices that create and expand democratic space in the North and East that could be of value to the rest of the country and lead to peace with justice. As an immediate step, embedding a broad notion of human security into political decision-making, building accountability and economic democracy into development initiatives1, linking a deeper vision of inter-ethnic and economic justice to peace process– needs to be priorities for this election.
