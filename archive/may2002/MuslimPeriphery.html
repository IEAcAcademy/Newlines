Articles · May 2002

Muslim on the Periphery

Ameena Hussein

That's what I call myself. Muslim on the Periphery. At one time in the old days it used to be my father's description of himself. He would sit at the head of the splendid ebony inlaid mahogany dining table surrounded by his four sons and his attentive wife. He would swill his glass of Beaujolais 1979 (it was a good year) holding it up occasionally to the light and watch the glints of ruby red wink at him through Baccarat crystal. "Muslim on the Periphery", he would say in a low voice still staring at the hypnotic swirls of the jewel-colored liquid. And then he would jerk up and glance at us and give a slightly embarrassed laugh and shrug almost in apology; a gesture so uniquely him and say "That's me children. That's me." And we his sons, who adored him, took pride at his peripharality and shushed our mother's horrified but muted protests at this almost sacrilegious state of affairs. Thus encouraged he would narrate a tale that would do justice to his membership on the fringe of the community. "When I was a magistrate in Galle", he would begin, " I had a case before me of infanticide. A young Muslim girl, no more than 18, was brought before me and charged with killing her newborn son. She was unmarried. Where is the father? I asked and they produced a proud thin squiggle of a man who looked at his daughter with ferocious sunken eyes. Not the father of the girl, I roared! The father of the child. The dead infant! The court looked bewildered as though I had asked after some figment of imagination. The prosecution and defense looked at one another agape. The Mudliyar realizing that something was amiss shushed the court sternly. I will not have this girl before me, I announced to the stunned court, until the father of the dead infant is brought before as well. This poor creature did not beget the child on her own. The fate of that child is therefore the responsibility of both. So until that young man is brought before me this child, (and now he looked dramatically and magnanimously at some imaginary girl before him), shall go free. After the session, many lawyers came to me and said waggling their fingers; the Muslim men won't like this new form of justice Mohamed. They will lynch you. Ah! Said I, but the Muslim women will erect a statue of my likeness and it will be worth it". He would end on a triumphant sigh and sink back into his chair. And we, ignorant of the reality of the situation that he was barely considered part of the community by both men and women, that he was derogatorily referred to as parangi maama, would beam with pride.

Today it is I who have stolen this description of my father's who no longer calls himself that. Instead he is a born again Muslim who makes five trips to the mosque a day and believes it a requirement to grow a straggly beard as far as it will go. My mother in the meantime is happier than before. She is under the impression that all her years of entreating the all Mighty has paid off. She is now in possession of a God fearing, five times a day praying, charity giving, Ramadan fasting, Hajj going, Muslim man. Unfortunately like some historical family curse it is my wife this time who rebels and protests at this inherited description of myself. During my idle moments I wonder if in time to come my son in turn will describe himself as so and if his wife (if he has one) will predictably take offence.

But I digress for at the moment it is I who am the Muslim on the periphery. I always wondered at my fellow Muslim brethren who embraced their muslimness with enthusiasm. I would see them wending their way towards the mosque for Jumma prayers, beggars and the rich alike. Thieves and men of God sitting side-by-side bowing down before the Almighty. Mumblers and Reciters releasing a torrent of words that surround them with a jist of meaning and nothing more. The sermon pounded into them by an over zealous believer was generally on something they couldn't care less about. And then after the prayers, the enthusiastic salaaming, hugging, and kissing that went on with all and sundry. I suppose I should mention that I don't attend these brotherhoods of man gatherings.

It is rare to find a Muslim who so publicly claims to be on the fringe of the Umma. There is a certain kind of toleration of the other kind of Muslim: those Muslim men (I specify men as it defies Muslim imagination to include women) who drink, gamble, smoke, dance, fornicate, and engage in other un-Islamic practices, as long as they present a facade of Muslimness. That they attend the Jumma prayers, observe the Ramadan fast, slaughter the Hajj goat, and wear the wedding Fez. I, however, have become an embarrassment. They don't know what to do with me. I have a bunch of academic letters behind my name that frightens them into silence. They dare not confront me with Godlessness for they know not what I will unleash upon them courtesy of my BA; JD; LLM; PHD. They seem to think that knowledge is power. Something on the lines of the pen is mightier than the sword. But that still does not stop a few of them from trying. They arrive at my doorstep - the long beards. And shove themselves through my hesitantly opened door. They sit uncomfortably perched on the edge of antique chairs and then they start their preaching. It is tiresome to listen to them. Those bores. My mind starts wandering and I begin to formulate silly edicts on the lines of - it should be made mandatory that those who insist on lecturing should do it as interestingly as possible. It should be deemed an offence to grow beards longer than six inches. Instead I politely nod and fix one of them with my eye. It always makes them uncomfortable. Direct gaze I mean. These fellows they are so loath to look one in the eye that they have a rather shifty look about them. It's a pity for it detracts greatly from their purpose. While the unfortunate victim of my direct gaze shifts about uncomfortably I let my mind wander through the universe entertaining myself with regurgitated tidbits of information. Once the monotonous drone of these preachers has ceased then I take my opportunity and hold forth on the injustice of the community. I rant with fervor on any particular topic that happens to pop into my mind regardless of whatever they said before. I have a few pet themes that I extopulate on. Intolerance; Rigidity; Female Suppression; Spirituality; Ritualism and other notions are part of my repertoire. I end with the resounding decision that ISLAM HAS FAILED! I watch as they catch their breath and look petrified at each other. I know that they now want to make their escape from this house of Shaitan, and permit myself an private titter as I watch them scramble up and charge towards the door in their eagerness to leave my house. I catch sight of my wife cowering in the back verandah fervently asking for forgiveness in the light of my behavior towards these men of God. I yell at the foolish woman and tell her that I want my lunch.

As I serve my stark meal of red rice, gotukola sambol and dhal I ruminate on the whole business of the Umma. This concept of the community escapes me. I cannot understand why one cannot be left alone to lead his or her life without embracing the whole citizenry of Islam. It must have been some fierce Karmic debt that I have to pay off in this life to be born into a religion as sociable as Islam while in comparison I have the personality of an eremite. There is no doubt that Islam is a religion for the gregarious. Group prayers, communal eating, collective ablutions exist to enforce the concept of community. I have an aversion to all of them. If I did pray I would rather do it in solitude, I am perfectly content to eat out of my own plate than with 5 other people from the same bucket and I am extremely fastidious about my personal ablutions. Alas! I do not seem to have the makings of a Muslim.

In this country of mine where my identity is tightly interwoven with my religion what happens to the likes of me? If I said I was a Sinhalese or a Tamil no person would question my religious affiliation. I would be free to be an atheist and yet have the comfort of belonging to a community. But by virtue of being a Muslim I am situated in a quandary. No practicing Muslim would call me a Muslim. The State insists that I identify myself as a Muslim only because my father is a Muslim (for in our patriarchal realm the mothers are only vessels of placenta). I am indifferent to the label knowing full well that I am Muslim only by virtue of birth. I resist qualifying my identity either as a "nominal Muslim" or a "non-practicing Muslim" on the grounds that as the State has assigned "Muslim" as my ethnic identity, that alone should suffice, with it being immaterial or irrelevant if I practice the religion or not. It cannot be denied that I am part of the Muslim cultural experience of Sri Lanka and thus I have created for myself the liberty of being Muslim in ethnic identity but not necessarily in religion. Therefore for others like me (for it is hard for me to believe that there aren't others like me) the State should create a slot that reads non-Muslim Muslim.

Obviously that is a ridiculous alternative for in short it is nobody's business if I am a practicing Muslim or not. The pillars of the Muslim community would be inclined to disagree I suspect. Recently as I thribbled through some ancient magazines I came across the Salman Rushdie fiasco. What a to do about less than nothing. Death to the apostate seems to be the Islamic decree. A trifle discomforting for would be apostates or in the closet apostates. Extremely unfair I would say to be born into a religion that does not allow for spiritual wanderings. If we are to go by the religious authorities, desertion of the faithful cannot be tolerated by Islam. A strange contradiction to occur in a religion that benevolently extols: There is no compulsion in religion. It smacks of schizophrenia does it not? Islam has become today, for I cannot believe that it always was so, a religion of intolerance. Any hint of diluting the religion with concepts from other philosophies is rebuffed sternly. And yet if any Muslim concerned about the purity or exclusivity of his religion read the Jewish and Christian documents they would find chunks of it reproduced in Islam. There is very little originality in the religion. The much coveted and safeguarded Sharia law is nothing but Jewish law modified here and mutated there.

I may have smiled during my silent and solitary cogitation during lunch and my wife taking it as a sign of atonement stops sulking and sits down to eat with me. I wisely kept my thoughts to myself. Peace and harmony is what I want in the household even though many people think to the contrary. I look at this woman seated before me. She is around 40ish and plump of face. Her sari potta is counter-weighted perfectly on her head. There are a few gray strands that lie on a bed of black. Her brow has never furrowed in deep thought and her mouth has never engaged in philosophical debates. She is a good woman and she must have endured many a sleepless night on my account. She is not the ideal partner for me, I must confess, but I have now grown accustomed to her and her ways. She does try so very hard.

That evening I prepare to go for a wedding. It is under duress that I attend, as Muslim weddings are primarily for women. I have in the past attended weddings where I have never seen the young woman who got married. Instead I was shoved into a hotel ballroom with a multitude of other men dressed in full suits, fed with alacrity and then as we have nothing else to do we go home. Many a time I have left my wife behind to find her own way home, as I have been unable to locate her in the segregated throng of the women's ballroom. Muslim weddings are boring affairs, an excuse for shallow people - both young and old to prance about and parade their latest acquisitions - both human and material. Children, spouses, jewelry and clothes are used as props in the dance for social attention.

My daughter, a bright young woman, confronts my criticisms with a theory. The Muslim wedding, she says, is part of our culture. It is an amalgamation of many wedding customs. It is a mishmash of Eastern, Western, Christian, Hindu customs enshrouded within a Muslim framework. She would have liked to include Buddhist as well but I know she couldn't find a ready example of Buddhism in our Muslim marriage mishmash and so she reluctantly omitted the majority religion from her argument. It is a good example of cultural acclimatization; she elaborates, that reflects the notion of multiculturalism. After all what better example can a minority population project in an effort to portray ethnic accommodation?

Ah! But I tell her, the Muslims get married in this particular way not for love of multiculturalism but for love of expenditure, tamasha and pomp and ceremony. They felt the basic Muslim marriage was slightly austere, not flamboyant enough. What kind of a marriage is this? They whined. Two male witnesses, husband to be, father-in-law to be, throng of male guests. Where was the prettified bride sat upon a throne, symbolizing topping on a cake ready to be devoured by her eager husband and his demanding family? What about fairy lights and ice sculptures, armloads of flowers and plump women swathed in rich embroidered saris? I snigger at my descriptions while my sweet daughter vainly tries to convince me. Oh! Daddy, it's a matter of perspective, she says, anything can be seen in a negative light. If the Muslims are not going to change isn't it worthwhile to reconstruct and interpret their world and life view in terms that would show them in a positive light? This way, both the Muslims and non-Muslims would feel some commonalities with each other. And I even got a Buddhist borrowing in the Muslim wedding, she finishes off triumphantly. It's the poruwa modified into a throne. Be it Buddhist poruwa or Hindu manavare it is nothing but hogwash and whitewash, I retort. If the Muslims of Sri Lanka even heard your analysis they would be appalled and repelled at the dilution of their marriage ceremonies. Today's Muslims are either fundamentalists or apologists. I am in the minority of Muslim realists. It was a religion that was introduced to a so-called savage people. It has Judeo-Christian roots and even resorted to violence when necessary. It was a religion that made no bones about the necessity of violence and was closer to Judaism in its eye for an eye principle than turn the other cheek Christianity. It was a realistic religion that allowed its followers to enjoy the goodies of this life and at the same time presented the opportunity for the faithful to look forward to similar worldly enjoyment in the hereafter. It advocated charity, mercy, empathy and respect and at the same time proffered an opportunity for men to exploit and suppress women. It has become a sour dour religion, which is one big men's club! My daughter is silent now and yet I am not deceived. I know she is thinking. And in some future time will hurl her newly refurbished theory at me in yet another attempt to see her religion in an acceptable light. For now, she murmurs that it does no good to advocate religious strife and dissent in this time and day. What did Salman Rushdie or Taslima Nasrin achieve in the long run she demands? They suffered the consequences but was it worth it in the end? I look at my beautiful spirited daughter and marvel at her ability to make me think. I appreciate her readiness to engage in debate a far cry from her brother a spineless shifty young man who is too much under his mother's influence. It would not surprise me the least if he went the long beards way. But I'm not done with the subject of marriages. Why can't they elope I retort childishly, and save all of us the bother of attending these tamashas. My wife frowns menacingly at me and I fade into a mumble. My daughter giggles at my contrived fear and I delight at my enjoyment of and with her. She has such a zest for life. What a pity that in the future she would most likely have to deal with some unsuitable man foistered on her whose main aim in life would be to squash her very spirit! What is a Muslim marriage? It is nothing but a contract with no religious ties whatsoever. During modern times with exposure to other communities the Muslims were attracted to the notion of till death do you apart, the sacredness of marriage and all the rest of the balderdash that accompanies it. Thus crept in a pseudo religious air liberally sprinkled with prayers, sermons, and blessings. In fact the Muslim marriage in my opinion is nothing but a transaction that legitimates the exchange of sexual services between a man and a woman. A Muslim man pays the meher to the woman and thus he buys her sexuality. If that is not so, pray explain how it is that once a woman withholds sex from her husband he is not obliged to maintain her. The religious requirement of mehr and the social requirement of dowry make one odd combination indeed. The woman sells her sexual services for a pittance and her very soul to buy the security of a man. I gaze at my daughter who unaware of my thoughts theorizes delightfully on. I realize I fear for her and suddenly feel very very old.

That night as my wife and I lay beside each other I reached out for her hand. She turned towards me willingly and we made the love of matrimony that has endured decades. I remembered the early days of marriage when she would resist disrobing entirely for the act. It's immodest, she would protest uncomfortably. It's shameful, she would insist. And I would try to have patience but very often lose it. Woman! I would shout, what the hell does it matter if you have clothes on or not. Your legs are still spread aren't they? Upon which she would weep and I would apologize. Do you have to be so hateful, she would sob? It's hard enough for me as it is. And I would be alerted immediately to her position. Sheltered young Muslim girl. Uneducated. Led into an arranged marriage with a man she had never seen or spoken with before her wedding day. Sexually ignorant or brainwashed by repressive religious teachers into believing that sex is repulsive. And suddenly upon marriage expected to behave like a sexual vixen. Abracadabra! I look at her as she sleeps heavily in the crook of my arm, her face in repose looks tranquil and serene and I cannot help but think I do not deserve her. Sleep eludes me and my mind begins to wander. Where do I fit in, I think and my mind hearkens to the past.

My own family is a mallung of Muslim misfits. But they have had the good sense to cloak their religious or social rovings. I remember my eldest brother, a delicate sensitive young man who spent much of his time sketching women's clothing. The ridicule that was heaped on his head by those around him, his forced early marriage, his realization of his attraction to other men, his failed attempts to salvage his life, drove him to drink and an early death. My third brother sought refuge in America, a country he now despises and can only tolerate by becoming a Hindu spiritual leader for the misguided. Ah! But he has the very good sense to present his Muslim face to his relatives. His hard currency spending makes the bitter pill sweeter to swallow on his occasional visits back to the fold. And the family has the cautionary sense to avoid visiting his home and thus confrontation of his obvious Hindu affiliation. Sometimes I admire his hypocrisy most times I do not. My youngest brother is a Sufi mureed. He has parked himself in the compound of a Sufi sheikh on the outskirts of Ajmer. With one exception we haven't seen him for thirty years. If I did I doubt that I would recognize him. The last time I saw him he refused to speak to any of us but merely sat in our respective verandahs swinging a thasbih uttering his zikr and contemplating. On evaluation, I would say that he is the best of us. There is a sense of peace and contentment that surrounds him. He asks for nothing, wants for nothing and I sense is barely of this world. And despite his foray into the mystical world of Islam he too is a misfit. After a week with us, he mercifully disappeared and I presume went back to his Sheikh in Ajmer.

This quest for identity on my part came about through sheer necessity. One does not live in a vacuum. At all times, in most situations, identities are constantly being constructed. They are presented and they are read. It is like a dance macabre. One day, one face. The next day, another. The Sri Lankan Muslim has always been in a state of confusion. They would like to think that pure Arab Hashemite blood is coursing through their veins. They claim that they are descended from the Arab traders who plied the oceans. Bowing to patriarchy they disregard the possible existence of Sinhala or Tamil mothers. They have looked towards the Arab states as their true homeland fantasizing and idealizing a concept of the Muslim Umma that is unrealistic. Notwithstanding that the largest Muslim population lies outside the Arab region, the idea of each and every one of them being Arabs in Sri Lankan skins is nourished and nurtured within and without. Each generation passionately identifies and empathizes with their unfortunate Muslim brothers and sisters be they in Palestine, Bosnia, Kosovo or Albania while ignoring their own countrymen Muslim or otherwise; East coast or West, Jaffna Muslims or Galle Muslims; Batticaloa Muslims or Colombo Muslims who have problems of their own. This synthetic sympathy serves them to salve their conscience without much effort. They are, in their reality, being good Muslims. Constantly seeing themselves as transient in their tropical homeland the Sri Lankan Muslim focuses his social and political attention on true good Muslim lands as a way of reaffirming his Muslim identity. In the meantime what has suffered is his Sri Lankan identity. As for me, it took temporary expulsion from Sri Lanka for the question of identity to come home to me. Removed from the complacence of my tolerant homeland I, still imbued with the remnants of the Sri Lankan Muslim Arab identity naturally gravitated towards the mosque of my new temporary home. There amidst the motley assortment of Indians, Pakistanis, Turks, Egyptians, and Arabs I expected a warm and genuine welcome from my rightful countrymen. However, soon enough I found my true place - right at the bottom of the pile. The scorn and contempt poured on my Sri Lankan head, the country of housemaids in their words, showed me the true colors of the Muslim Umma. The Arabs were under the impression that they were the true and only inheritors of Islam. The Egyptians called themselves Africans sometimes and Arabs at other times, the Indians and Pakistanis engaged in mini power struggles from time to time and thus for much of the time pretty much ignored the others. In disgust at this un-Islamic display of Muslim brotherhood I then began to undergo the laborious process of creating an identity for myself.
I have always been in a precarious position. One that straddles the identities of Muslim and Sri Lankan trying to find, constantly searching a perfect formula that will allow me to hold both identities close to my heart. The key is acceptance I believe. The knowledge that my created identity sits comfortably with others. Unfortunately, I have to admit failure there. The curious thing is that Muslims see me as not one of them, Sri Lankans (the majority of them in any case) sees me as Muslim and there you have it. A Muslim on the Periphery of all worlds.

Ameena Hussein is an Executive Assistant for International Center for Human Rights Policy, Geneva, Switzerland.
