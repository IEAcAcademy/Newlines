Minor Matters

Qadri Ismail

Living in Another Country

My mother always told me this was not our country.

Not every day. Perhaps not even once a week. How often, I don't remember. But regularly enough for the thought to register.

I was born here, I would usually counter. You were born here. How can this not be our country? This is their country, she would respond, tryingly. Don't make a noise about it. We should be glad the Sinhalese are tolerating us.

The conversation usually ended there. Sometimes, though, I would pursue the proposition. If not this where, I would ask. What is our country? This question was rarely answered. If I kept pushing, I heard Pakistan; occasionally, Egypt or Saudi Arabia; once, Malaysia. Most often: I don't know what our country is, but Ceylon is not.

Many years - and fatigued gray cells - later, I would categorize my mother's statement as a classic instance of a minority worldview.

Minor. Check the word in any dictionary and this is what you get: lesser, subordinate, inconsiderable, inferior, insignificant, not adult. Not very good things to be.

And yet, G.L.Pieris is of the opinion that there could be dignity in being these things. He told a seminar recently that the rights of minorities were a matter of entitlement, not something that should be dependent upon the majority's goodwill; that these rights must be constitutionally guaranteed; otherwise, the minorities cannot live with dignity. This is a huge advance from the pose of previous Sri Lankan governments. The Jayewardene regime believed in simply terrorizing its minorities. Premadasa at least got his rhetoric right (at some points in time). This government seems prepared to translate its declarations into decrees (at some point in time).

But based upon a founding principle that's fundamentally flawed. Because the principle in effect is that some Sri Lankans will always be subordinate to others. Therefore I hope the powerful professor will consider this: there is no dignity in being minor, lesser, insignificant. Indeed, as long as you think there are minorities in this country, and act accordingly, we cannot and will not have peace.

*

For "minority" is predicated upon the existence of another term, "majority". We have internalized the idea that these terms are mere reflections of numbers, of bodies on the ground; we think the term "Sinhala majority" is a fact of nature. But the significance of numbers is not transparent or self-evident; numbers do not speak. They have meaning thrust upon them.

I offer as evidence a statement not from an anti-racist, but from that subtlest of champions of Sinhala dominance, the historian Kingsley Muthumani De Silva. In an article published in 1986, he wrote that, upto the early twentieth century, "the Tamils…were not regarded as a minority community, but were treated as one of two majority groups". If you don't want to believe Professor de Silva, check the newspapers of the time; they will confirm his view.

What is one to make of this? Especially given that, between then and now, the proportion of Sinhalese to the rest of the population has not radically altered. How come we once had two major communities? How come the same numbers mean different things at different times?

The simple answer is the Donoughmore reforms, which introduced Westminster-style winner-take-all parliamentary democracy to Sri Lanka. Yes, democracy - or at least our brand of it - produced our problems. For it ensures that there will be just one majority which will have its way. Thus, after Donoughmore, the Tamils (slowly) lost their equal status. The Sinhalese became the singular, exclusive majority community; and (quickly) an exclusivist one. Indeed, the entirety of subsequent Tamil political demands, from fifty-fifty through federalism to separatism, could be understood as a response to minority status.

*

Turn that dictionary to a few pages before minor and this is what you get: major equals greater, superior, principal, important, dominant. A majority, in other words, isn't a purely descriptive term, an innocent reflection of number, the largest group of people. It implies certain rights, if not privileges; including that of domination.

For that is what majorities do: dominate. They may accomplish this brazenly or subtly, in the face of resistance or acquiescence. But, whether it is men against women, heterosexuals against homosexuals, Govigama against the other castes, right-handers versus the left or Sinhalese versus the rest, all majorities dominate.

A majority, therefore, is not about number but about power. The power to dominate, to impose its norms and desires on others. Consequently, if one is a devotee of democracy, one cannot like majorities. Not those of a permanent kind; not the concept, either.

*

This may appear counter-intuitive. How else can there be democracy, you may ask. I am not convinced I know the answer. But political science has produced an idea, one way of grappling with the problem that we should consider: consociationalism, meaning an "association of communities". Such an idea could be deployed, in the Sri Lankan instance, not just to achieve autonomy in the regions, but to prevent dominance by and in the centre too. Deployed to ensure that the majority cannot do anything without the consent of the minorities. Deployed, in short, to eventually do away with the very notion of a majority.
Such a political system will not go down well with (majoritarian) Sinhala nationalism. But then, this nationalism has been and still is the primary threat to reconciliation in this country. Without marginalizing this ideology, we will only have domination - not peace.

*

The German philosopher Theodor Adorno once defined peace as "a state of distinctness without domination". This is a careful formulation that our constitution changers would do well to consider. For, Adorno does not deploy that trendy term, difference - which implies a norm, a point of reference one is different from. Distinctness tries to conceive of a society outside the logic of domination.

The institutional arrangements through which such a society would function must, of course, be negotiated between all parties concerned. But I can offer some ideas about the symbols to represent the new country. They cannot, obviously, denote domination by any group.

In a peaceful country, Buddhism cannot be granted the "foremost" place by the constitution. Indeed, Sri Lanka - if that name will continue - must be made a secular entity, and the state should stop bankrolling organized monks, ministers and mullahs. In a peaceful country, the so-called national anthem will also have to be rewritten - with verses in Sinhala and Tamil (and perhaps even English); or, maybe, like South Africa we could have two anthems.

And then there is that truly awful flag, with that gross Sinhala Lion not just dominating its dimensions but actually directing its sword at the stripes representing the minorities. The new flag may eventually consist of a stripe of equal size for every group in the country. But let a committee decide what ultimate shape it will take. In the interim, though, I suggest we trash the lion and adopt a design of plain white. This will not only signify that Sri Lanka has again become a peaceful place; but, since white is also the colour of mourning, it will remind us of those thousands who died - some violently, others accidentally, most unnecessarily - on the path to that peace.
